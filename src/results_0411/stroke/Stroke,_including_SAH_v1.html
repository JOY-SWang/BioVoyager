Introduction

Stroke (Disease code: I9_STROKE) is an acute cerebrovascular disorder caused by interruption of cerebral blood flow or rupture of cerebral vessels, leading to rapid neurological injury through ischemia, hemorrhage, inflammation, and tissue remodeling. In the analysis of the UK Biobank Pharma Proteomics Project data, circulating levels of 175 plasma proteins were significantly associated with stroke, based on 50,797 controls and 1,392 cases after Bonferroni correction at P < 0.000000001. Understanding the biological mechanisms represented by these 175 proteins is essential for clarifying the molecular basis of stroke, because the disease reflects not only vascular occlusion or bleeding but also a coordinated systemic response involving immune activation, extracellular matrix injury, cytokine signaling, and cellular stress adaptation. We therefore performed a comprehensive functional enrichment analysis on the 175 proteins to delineate the biological landscape of stroke, screening pathways from multiple resources, including Gene Ontology Molecular Function, Gene Ontology Biological Process, Gene Ontology Cellular Component, Reactome, and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes. Across these resources, we identified 70 significantly enriched biological pathways (Table 1). The enriched pathways reveal a molecular profile strongly centered on inflammatory and immune regulation, with particularly strong statistical support for cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction at P = 5.315 × 10⁻¹⁶, external encapsulating structure at P = 9.273 × 10⁻¹⁷, tumor necrosis factors bind their physiological receptors at P = 5.548 × 10⁻¹⁰, and regulation of leukocyte activation at P = 1.028 × 10⁻⁸. In parallel, the analysis highlights extracellular matrix and tissue barrier processes, including extracellular matrix structural constituent at P = 3.811 × 10⁻⁸ and external encapsulating structure with 33 associated proteins, together supporting a substantial contribution of neurovascular structural remodeling to stroke biology. Additional enrichment of mitogen-activated protein kinase signaling at P = 3.673 × 10⁻⁴ and interleukin ten signaling at P = 9.391 × 10⁻⁵ further indicates that stress-response signaling and counter-regulatory immune pathways are also important components of the stroke-associated proteomic signature. Collectively, these findings suggest that stroke is characterized by an integrated molecular framework involving cytokine signaling, leukocyte activation, extracellular structural disruption, protease regulation, and adaptive stress signaling. In the following sections, we examine the most significant pathways and their associated protein interactions in greater detail.

Overall, the pathway landscape is consistent with a model in which stroke is associated with coordinated inflammatory activation, cytokine signaling, extracellular matrix injury, and engagement of stress-response pathways. HRD1 complex (CORUM:6859)

The HMG CoA reductase degradation protein 1 complex is a core endoplasmic reticulum associated degradation machinery that preserves protein quality control within the endoplasmic reticulum by identifying misfolded or incompletely folded proteins, moving them across the endoplasmic reticulum membrane back into the cytosol, attaching ubiquitin, and directing them to proteasomal destruction. Structural and mechanistic studies indicate that this complex is organized around the ubiquitin ligase HMG CoA reductase degradation protein 1 together with accessory factors that mediate substrate recognition, channel formation, and degradation efficiency. In yeast, the active complex includes HMG CoA reductase degradation protein 1, HMG CoA reductase degradation protein 3, degradation in endoplasmic reticulum protein 1, ubiquitin suppressor protein 1, and yeast oligosaccharyltransferase 9, which together create a luminal recognition site for misfolded glycoproteins and a membrane retrotranslocation route formed by paired half channels in a locally thinned membrane region, thereby allowing hydrophilic polypeptide segments to cross the membrane for degradation [1] [2] [3]. In mammals, the most conserved functional counterpart appears to be the suppressor of lin 12 like and HMG CoA reductase degradation protein 1 complex, in which suppressor of lin 12 like is required for stable complex assembly, substrate recruitment, and full ligase activity. Genetic and biochemical studies further suggest that disruption of suppressor of lin 12 like and HMG CoA reductase degradation protein 1 coupling impairs endoplasmic reticulum associated degradation at multiple levels, including substrate handoff, complex formation, and recruitment of ubiquitin conjugating enzymes, and that such defects may compromise organismal viability and contribute to human neurodevelopmental disease [4] [5] [6]. Beyond proteostasis, this pathway also appears to modulate inflammatory signaling by targeting newly synthesized stimulator of interferon genes for degradation under basal conditions, thereby limiting the size of the activable innate immune pool [7]. Taken together, the HMG CoA reductase degradation protein 1 complex may be viewed as a proteostasis and signaling checkpoint that integrates endoplasmic reticulum homeostasis, degradation of misfolded proteins, and restraint of maladaptive inflammatory activation.

Against this mechanistic background, the relevance of the HMG CoA reductase degradation protein 1 complex to stroke can be considered in the context of ischemic and reperfusion stress, during which neurons, glia, and vascular cells experience abrupt disturbances in oxygen supply, energy metabolism, calcium balance, redox state, and protein folding. Although most cited studies define the pathway mechanistically rather than in stroke models, they provide a biologically plausible framework for stroke relevance because failure of suppressor of lin 12 like and HMG CoA reductase degradation protein 1 mediated endoplasmic reticulum associated degradation causes accumulation of misfolded proteins and heightened cellular stress, processes that are highly pertinent to ischemic brain injury [1] [2] [4] [5]. Human and mouse genetic evidence further indicates that reduced function of this pathway is especially damaging to the nervous system, producing developmental delay, ataxia, and loss of viability, which supports the idea that neural tissue is highly dependent on intact endoplasmic reticulum associated degradation capacity [6] [4]. Most directly, a recent stroke focused study in a chronic kidney disease and stroke model reported that decreased suppressor of lin 12 like expression was associated with altered endoplasmic reticulum homeostasis, enhanced endoplasmic reticulum stress, greater cellular death, neurodegeneration, and worsened neurological and cognitive outcomes, while pharmacological inhibition of endoplasmic reticulum stress ameliorated these abnormalities and reduced white matter damage, thereby placing impaired suppressor of lin 12 like and HMG CoA reductase degradation protein 1 function within a plausible causal chain linking proteostasis failure to aggravated stroke injury [8]. In parallel, the finding that suppressor of lin 12 like and HMG CoA reductase degradation protein 1 limits stimulator of interferon genes signaling suggests an additional stroke relevant mechanism, because loss of this degradative brake could amplify innate immune and inflammatory responses after ischemia and thereby potentially exacerbate tissue injury and secondary degeneration [7]. Overall, the current literature supports the view that the HMG CoA reductase degradation protein 1 complex is related to stroke primarily as a protective endoplasmic reticulum quality control pathway whose dysfunction may intensify ischemia induced proteotoxic stress, inflammatory activation, white matter injury, and post stroke functional decline, particularly in the presence of systemic comorbidities such as chronic kidney disease.[1] [2] [3] [6] [4] [5] [7] [8]

antigen processing and presentation of peptide antigen via MHC class I (GO:0002474)

Antigen processing and presentation of peptide antigen via major histocompatibility complex class one is a core cellular immune pathway through which intracellular proteins are degraded into short peptides, loaded onto classical major histocompatibility complex class one molecules, and displayed at the cell surface for surveillance by cluster of differentiation eight positive cytotoxic T lymphocytes. In the canonical route, endogenous proteins are cleaved mainly by the proteasome, including the immunoproteasome under inflammatory conditions, and the resulting peptides are transported into the endoplasmic reticulum by transporter associated with antigen processing, where peptide loading complexes support assembly of major histocompatibility complex class one heavy chain with beta two microglobulin and antigenic peptide before export through the Golgi apparatus to the plasma membrane. The pathway is highly regulated at multiple levels, including transcriptional control of major histocompatibility complex class one molecules and antigen processing machinery by inflammatory signaling such as nuclear factor kappa B and histone acetyltransferases p300 and CREB binding protein, as demonstrated in cancer cells where activation of these regulators increases major histocompatibility complex class one expression, immunoproteasome components, and peptide transport machinery [14]. Beyond direct presentation of endogenous antigens, professional antigen presenting cells, especially dendritic cells, can route exogenous material into major histocompatibility complex class one presentation through cross-presentation, using either cytosolic or vacuolar processing routes and specialized intracellular trafficking steps involving post Golgi compartments and phagosomal export machinery [15] [16] [17] [18] [19]. The pathway is also sensitive to perturbation by infection, cellular stress, and organelle dysfunction: viral proteins can suppress peptide loading or surface display, whereas endoplasmic reticulum stress and Golgi fragmentation can reduce peptide major histocompatibility complex class one complex presentation [20] [21] [22]. Collectively, this pathway links intracellular proteostasis, inflammatory signaling, organelle homeostasis, and adaptive immune activation, thereby shaping whether damaged, infected, transformed, or stressed cells become visible to cytotoxic lymphocytes.

Against this mechanistic background, the pathway may be cautiously considered in relation to stroke, where sterile injury and immune activation occur in parallel over time. Although the provided publications do not study stroke directly, they define mechanisms that are highly relevant to stroke pathobiology because stroke induces sterile inflammation, neuronal and vascular cell stress, blood brain barrier disruption, antigen release, and subsequent activation of both innate and adaptive immunity. In ischemic or hemorrhagic brain injury, damaged neurons, astrocytes, oligodendrocytes, endothelial cells, microglia, infiltrating macrophages, and dendritic cell like antigen presenting cells are exposed to inflammatory cytokines and danger signals that are expected to enhance major histocompatibility complex class one antigen processing and presentation machinery, particularly through pathways involving nuclear factor kappa B, signal transducer and activator of transcription one, and immunoproteasome induction. This inference is supported by studies showing that inflammatory transcriptional programs can strongly increase major histocompatibility complex class one antigen presentation capacity, including upregulation of major histocompatibility complex class one molecules, immunoproteasome subunits, and peptide transport machinery [14] [23] [24]. In the post stroke brain, such enhancement would be expected to increase display of self peptides derived from injured intracellular proteins, thereby facilitating recognition by cytotoxic T lymphocytes and potentially amplifying secondary tissue injury. At the same time, cross-presentation mechanisms characterized in dendritic cells provide a plausible route by which brain derived antigens released from dying cells are captured in cervical lymphoid tissues or by infiltrating antigen presenting cells and then presented on major histocompatibility complex class one to prime cytotoxic T cell responses [15] [16] [17] [18] [19]. This process is particularly relevant to delayed neuroinflammation after stroke, when peripheral adaptive immunity contributes to lesion evolution and repair. The publications also indicate that endoplasmic reticulum stress and Golgi disorganization suppress major histocompatibility complex class one peptide display [21], a finding of special importance in stroke because ischemia, reperfusion, oxidative stress, and excitotoxicity profoundly disturb endoplasmic reticulum and Golgi homeostasis; thus, stroke may produce spatially and temporally heterogeneous antigen presentation, with some cells becoming more immunogenic through inflammatory induction while others lose efficient peptide loading because of severe organelle injury. In addition, studies of viral immune evasion and peptide loading complex arrest illustrate how disruption of transporter associated with antigen processing dependent loading changes cell surface major histocompatibility complex class one expression [22] [20], which is conceptually relevant because similar defects could emerge in severely injured brain cells after stroke. Overall, this pathway is related to stroke through its central role in converting intracellular damage caused by cerebral ischemia or hemorrhage into adaptive immune recognition: it can promote cytotoxic lymphocyte activation against brain antigens, shape the magnitude and persistence of post stroke neuroinflammation, and influence the balance between harmful immune mediated secondary injury and beneficial clearance of damaged tissue.[14] [15] [16] [24] [22] [17] [18] [23] [20] [21] [19]

Appendix; goblet cells[≥Low] (HPA:0030891)

The pathway term "Appendix; goblet cells[greater than or equal to Low]" most plausibly refers to the presence or relative abundance of goblet cells within appendiceal mucosa at or above a low threshold. Goblet cells are specialized secretory epithelial cells distributed throughout the intestinal tract, including the appendix, and their principal function is the production and secretion of mucins that form the protective mucus layer overlying the intestinal epithelium. This mucus barrier limits direct contact between luminal microbes and the mucosal surface, supports epithelial integrity, and contributes to immune homeostasis. In the appendix, goblet cells are a prominent epithelial lineage and are relevant both to normal barrier physiology and to appendiceal pathological states, including mucinous neoplasms and goblet cell adenocarcinoma. Although most of the provided literature on appendiceal goblet cells concerns neoplastic classification rather than cerebrovascular disease, the broader biological role of goblet cells in maintaining intestinal barrier function appears directly relevant to stroke because intestinal barrier disruption after cerebral ischemia may amplify systemic inflammation. Thus, this pathway may be interpreted less as an appendix-specific oncological marker in stroke and more as a surrogate of mucin-producing epithelial barrier competence within the gut-associated immune axis.

Building on this interpretation, the available evidence suggests that the relationship between goblet cells and stroke is mediated through the gut barrier and systemic inflammation rather than through appendiceal neoplasia. Among the provided studies, the most directly relevant report demonstrated in an experimental model of ischemic stroke that stroke increased gut mast cells, enhanced histamine signaling, elevated circulating inflammatory mediators, and was accompanied by a significant reduction in mucin-producing goblet cells together with loss of gut barrier integrity; these effects were especially pronounced in aged animals, thereby linking depletion of goblet cells to post-stroke intestinal inflammation and peripheral immune activation [25]. This finding is biologically important because goblet cells sustain the mucus layer that restrains microbial and inflammatory translocation from the intestinal lumen into the circulation. Therefore, a pathway reflecting goblet cell presence in the appendix or gut mucosa may be relevant to stroke as an indicator of preserved mucosal defense, whereas reduced goblet cell abundance would be expected to favor barrier failure, exaggerated systemic cytokine release, microbiota disturbance, and potentially worse neurological recovery after ischemic injury. By contrast, the remaining publications primarily describe goblet cell adenocarcinoma and other appendiceal neoplasms, which clarify the epithelial identity and pathological significance of appendiceal goblet cells but do not provide direct evidence for a causal role in stroke. Taken together, the literature supports a stroke connection for this pathway through post-ischemic gut inflammation, loss of mucin-producing goblet cells, and consequent impairment of intestinal barrier function, rather than through appendix-specific tumor biology.[25]

endopeptidase inhibitor activity (GO:0004866)

Endopeptidase inhibitor activity refers to the molecular function by which a protein, peptide, antibody, or small molecule binds to an endopeptidase and suppresses its catalytic activity. Endopeptidases cleave peptide bonds within target proteins and thereby regulate coagulation, fibrinolysis, extracellular matrix turnover, inflammatory signaling, blood vessel integrity, and cell death. In the context of cerebrovascular disease, this activity is especially important because several biologically central enzymes in stroke are endopeptidases, including coagulation proteases such as activated factor eleven and thrombin, fibrinolytic proteases such as tissue plasminogen activator, and matrix metalloproteinases such as matrix metalloproteinase three. Endogenous inhibitors within this functional space include serpin family members such as plasminogen activator inhibitor one, neuroserpin, antithrombin, and complement-related protease inhibitors, whereas therapeutic inhibitors include monoclonal antibodies, direct oral anticoagulants, reversal agents, and selective metalloproteinase inhibitors. The pathway is therefore not a single linear cascade, but rather a broader regulatory principle that restrains proteolysis in blood and brain. In stroke biology, this balance is crucial because insufficient inhibition of endopeptidases can promote thrombosis, blood brain barrier disruption, hemorrhagic transformation, and neuroinflammation, whereas excessive inhibition can impair thrombus resolution or alter hemostatic protection. Accordingly, endopeptidase inhibitor activity has relevance to both ischemic stroke prevention and acute or subacute brain injury after stroke.

Against this mechanistic background, the published literature collectively suggests that endopeptidase inhibitor activity is closely linked to stroke through two major and partially opposing mechanisms: regulation of thrombus formation in the circulation and limitation of protease-mediated injury within the ischemic brain. On the prevention side, inhibition of coagulation endopeptidases modifies cardioembolic stroke risk in atrial fibrillation. Activated factor eleven inhibition reduced bleeding but did not provide equivalent protection from stroke compared with established anticoagulation, as shown by the premature termination of the asundexian trial because stroke or systemic embolism was more frequent with asundexian than with apixaban, despite fewer major bleeding events, and similar patterns were observed across prior oral anticoagulant exposure strata, indicating that selective suppression of this protease may alter the efficacy-safety balance in stroke prevention rather than simply improve it [63] [64]. By contrast, inhibition of factor eleven with abelacimab markedly lowered bleeding relative to rivaroxaban, supporting the concept that targeting coagulation proteases remains mechanistically important for stroke prevention, although definitive protection from ischemic events still appears to require careful validation [65].

A complementary line of evidence points from the circulation to the neurovascular unit, where endopeptidase inhibitor activity may be protective when it restrains proteases that damage the blood brain barrier or amplify inflammatory injury. Matrix metalloproteinase three, a zinc-dependent endopeptidase, was identified as a mediator of vascular injury, edema, and hemorrhagic transformation, particularly in hyperglycemic and diabetic stroke models, and its inhibition improved functional outcome and reduced vascular damage, highlighting the potential role of protease inhibition in preserving cerebrovascular integrity after ischemia and reperfusion (Stroke abstracts on matrix metalloproteinase three inhibition; PubMed:39419277). A particularly strong example is the compartmentalized control of tissue plasminogen activator by distinct endogenous inhibitors: neuroserpin limits harmful tissue plasminogen activator activity in the central nervous system and preserves blood brain barrier function, whereas plasminogen activator inhibitor one restrains intravascular fibrinolysis. Experimental evidence showed that neuroserpin deficiency worsened infarct size, blood brain barrier permeability, and spontaneous intracerebral hemorrhage, while plasminogen activator inhibitor one deficiency improved cerebral blood flow recovery and reduced infarct size, demonstrating that the biological effect of endopeptidase inhibition depends on where the target protease acts [66]. Reviews further support neuroserpin as a neuroprotective inhibitor that may extend the safe therapeutic window of thrombolysis and reduce inflammation and barrier disruption after stroke [67] [68]. Clinical studies on idarucizumab, a reversal agent for dabigatran, also illustrate the translational importance of controlling endopeptidase-related coagulation pathways in acute stroke care, because rapid neutralization of anticoagulant effect can restore eligibility for thrombolysis in ischemic stroke and may improve management of hemorrhagic presentations [69] [70]. More broadly, systematic synthesis of serpin studies concluded that serpin family proteins are altered in stroke patients and that several protease inhibitors show therapeutic benefit in experimental ischemic stroke, reinforcing the view that endopeptidase inhibitor activity is a central determinant of stroke onset, progression, and treatment response [71].[63] [64] [65] [72] [66] [67] [68] [69] [70] [71]

Colon; goblet cells[High] (HPA:0130893)

The pathway denoted as colon goblet cells high refers to a transcriptionally and functionally enriched state of colonic goblet cells, which are specialized secretory epithelial cells responsible for producing mucins that organize the colonic mucus barrier. Interleukin ten signaling is a canonical anti-inflammatory and immunoregulatory cytokine pathway that limits excessive innate and adaptive immune activation while supporting tissue protection and repair. Because stroke evolves through temporally dynamic phases that include acute ischemic injury, inflammatory expansion, tissue clearance, and later remodeling, interleukin ten signaling is perhaps best understood as a context-dependent protective pathway whose beneficial effects may depend on timing, cellular source, target cell population, and the balance between local neuroprotection and systemic immunosuppression.

Against this biological background, the collected literature suggests that interleukin ten signaling is closely related to stroke because it functions as a major endogenous brake on post-stroke inflammation while also contributing to repair and recovery. The description provided indicates that ANGPT2 modulates ANGPT1-TEK signaling, can compete for the same binding site, and influences phosphoinositide three-kinase-protein kinase B and focal adhesion kinase-extracellular signal-regulated kinase pathways, all of which are mechanistically linked to endothelial survival and barrier integrity. Thus, ANGPT2 stands out as a plausible mediator of ischemia-induced vascular destabilization as well as later-stage neovascular remodeling, two hallmark features of stroke pathobiology.

Closely related to this vascular remodeling theme, EGFL7 is notable in stroke because it is a vascular developmental and remodeling factor with direct effects on endothelial organization and vessel formation. Following cerebral ischemia, restoration of microvascular integrity and re-establishment of perfusion are critical determinants of tissue salvage and neurological recovery. EGFL7-mediated vascular tubulogenesis and endothelial adhesion suggest a role in rebuilding damaged vascular networks after ischemic injury. Its inhibition of smooth muscle cell migration may also influence vascular wall remodeling and possibly limit maladaptive responses in injured vessels. Because stroke recovery depends in part on coordinated angiogenesis and stabilization of the neurovascular unit, EGFL7 is particularly relevant as a candidate regulator of post-stroke vascular repair.

In a similar manner, IGFBP7 is of particular interest in stroke because its annotated functions converge on vascular homeostasis, endothelial biology, and angiogenesis. Prostacyclin production is especially relevant, as prostacyclin is vasoprotective, anti-platelet, and anti-inflammatory, all of which are highly pertinent to ischemic cerebrovascular injury. In addition, the recently described IGFBP7-CD93 interaction in angiogenesis suggests a role in endothelial remodeling and revascularization after injury. Since stroke outcome is strongly influenced by microvascular patency, endothelial activation, and the balance between thrombosis and repair, IGFBP7 emerges as a potentially meaningful mediator linking vascular signaling to both acute ischemic damage and subsequent recovery.[169]

Cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction (KEGG:04060)

The cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction pathway is a core intercellular communication network that governs how immune, vascular, glial, and neural cells exchange inflammatory, reparative, and homeostatic signals. It comprises soluble ligands such as interleukins, chemokines, interferons, tumor necrosis factor family members, colony-stimulating factors, and growth factors, together with their cognate membrane receptors. Through ligand-receptor binding, this pathway initiates downstream signaling programs that regulate leukocyte recruitment, endothelial activation, blood-brain barrier integrity, microglial and macrophage polarization, neuronal survival or death, angiogenesis, thromboinflammation, and tissue remodeling. In the setting of cerebral ischemia, cytokine-receptor signaling is rapidly induced both within the ischemic brain and in the peripheral immune compartment. This pathway is therefore not a single linear cascade, but rather a signaling interface that integrates multiple biological processes central to stroke pathogenesis, including sterile inflammation, innate and adaptive immune activation, chemotaxis, vascular dysfunction, and post-ischemic repair. The publications provided consistently identify this pathway as one of the most significantly enriched pathways in ischemic stroke transcriptomic, epigenetic, non-coding ribonucleic acid, and single-cell analyses, indicating that dysregulated cytokine-receptor communication is a recurrent systems-level feature of stroke biology.

Building on this general framework, the studies provided further suggest that the cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction pathway may represent a recurrent molecular signature linking ischemic stroke to inflammatory injury, immune cell recruitment, neurovascular dysfunction, and subsequent repair. Multiple blood-based and tissue-level transcriptomic analyses identified this pathway among the most significantly enriched pathways in ischemic stroke, often alongside tumor necrosis factor signaling, nuclear factor kappa B signaling, transforming growth factor beta signaling, and chemokine signaling, which together suggest that stroke induces a broad inflammatory communication program rather than isolated gene changes. Mechanistically, the pathway appears to contribute to stroke through several interconnected processes. First, it may amplify acute inflammation: key mediators such as tumor necrosis factor, interleukin 6, C-X-C motif chemokine ligand 8, and C-C motif chemokine ligand 2 were repeatedly implicated as hub or differentially expressed genes, and experimental inhibition of C-C motif chemokine ligand 2 reduced infarct size, neurological impairment, and expression of related inflammatory proteins, supporting a possible causal contribution of chemokine-driven injury. Second, it appears to shape immune cell behavior and cellular cross-talk: single-cell analysis showed enrichment of lactylation-related programs in natural killer cells and T cells, with predicted signaling to neural cells through tumor necrosis factor family ligand-receptor pairs, while other studies linked the pathway to immune infiltration patterns, microglial polarization, and sex-specific immune responses. Third, it may also be connected to vascular adaptation and repair, because angiogenesis-focused transcriptomic work in cerebral ischemia identified this pathway within competing endogenous ribonucleic acid networks that may regulate post-ischemic neovascularization. Fourth, the pathway appears to be embedded in higher-order regulatory layers, including circular ribonucleic acids, micro ribonucleic acids, messenger ribonucleic acid methylation, and methyltransferase-dependent methylation control, all of which converged on cytokine-receptor signaling as a major enriched process in stroke. Finally, several integrative network studies proposed therapeutically relevant nodes within this pathway, including tumor necrosis factor, interleukin 6, vascular endothelial growth factor A, angiotensin receptor type 1, interleukin 7 receptor, and C-C chemokine receptor type 7, suggesting that modulation of cytokine-receptor communication may have diagnostic, prognostic, and therapeutic value. Taken together, the available evidence suggests that the cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction pathway may be centrally involved in the initiation and propagation of post-ischemic inflammation, the recruitment and activation of peripheral and resident immune cells, the regulation of neurovascular injury, and the orchestration of endogenous recovery responses in stroke.[203] [204] [205] [206] [207] [208] [209] [210] [211] [212] [213] [214] [215] [216] [217] [218]
