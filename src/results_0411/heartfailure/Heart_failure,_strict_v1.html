Introduction

Heart failure is a progressive clinical syndrome in which the heart cannot maintain sufficient blood flow to meet systemic metabolic demands, leading to impaired organ perfusion and congestion (Disease code: heartfailure). In the United Kingdom Biobank Pharma Proteomics Project dataset, circulating levels of 678 plasma proteins were significantly associated with heart failure, based on an analysis including 50,331 controls and 2,263 cases under a Bonferroni-corrected significance threshold of P < 1 × 10⁻⁹. Understanding the biological mechanisms represented by these 678 proteins is essential for clarifying the molecular basis of heart failure, because this syndrome arises from the convergence of structural remodeling, inflammatory activation, vascular dysfunction, metabolic stress, and progressive loss of cardiac reserve. To define these mechanisms systematically, we performed a comprehensive functional enrichment analysis of the 678 proteins across 104 analyzed pathways using multiple pathway resources, including Gene Ontology Molecular Function, Gene Ontology Biological Process, Gene Ontology Cellular Component, Reactome, and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes. We identified 104 significantly enriched biological pathways (Table 1), indicating broad and coordinated biological disruption across the heart failure-associated protein landscape. The enriched results revealed strong representation of extracellular matrix and external structural biology, proteolytic activity, cell adhesion, cytokine-mediated signaling, humoral and innate immune processes, antigen presentation, receptor complex organization, and amyloid-related disease mechanisms. Several of the most statistically significant signals converged on extracellular and matrix remodeling processes, including external encapsulating structure with P < 4.277 × 10⁻⁴⁸ and extracellular matrix organization with P < 4.762 × 10⁻²², while immune and inflammatory pathways were also prominent, including cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction with P < 3.432 × 10⁻⁴⁴, humoral immune response with P < 7.564 × 10⁻²⁰, receptor complex with P < 6.484 × 10⁻¹⁷, and neutrophil degranulation with P < 4.509 × 10⁻¹². Additional enrichment of glycosaminoglycan binding with P < 1.284 × 10⁻¹⁵, endopeptidase activity with P < 7.226 × 10⁻¹⁴, cell adhesion molecules with P < 1.275 × 10⁻⁸, and amyloidosis with P < 8.085 × 10⁻⁴ further supports a disease framework shaped by extracellular remodeling, inflammatory signaling, vascular and endothelial perturbation, and infiltrative or proteostatic pathology. Together, these pathways provide a quantitative and biologically coherent view of heart failure as a multisystem molecular disorder, and the following sections examine the most informative pathways and protein interaction patterns in greater detail.

Colon; goblet cells[≥Low] (HPA:0130891)

This pathway refers to a low abundance state of colonic goblet cells, the specialized secretory epithelial cells that synthesize and release mucins, especially mucin 2, together with trefoil factors and other antimicrobial or barrier-supporting molecules. In the healthy colon, goblet cells generate the mucus layers that physically separate luminal microorganisms from the epithelial surface and thereby preserve intestinal barrier integrity, regulate host microbial contact, and limit inflammatory activation. The cited literature shows that loss or reduction of goblet cells disrupts the inner mucus layer, increases bacterial translocation into colonic tissue, and is associated with heightened local and systemic inflammatory signals, including interferon gamma and interleukin 6, as demonstrated in graft-versus-host disease models where goblet cell preservation improved barrier function and reduced inflammation [1]. Multiple studies further indicate that goblet cell differentiation and maintenance are controlled by developmental and epithelial programs such as Notch signaling, bone morphogenetic protein signaling, dedicator of cytokinesis 4, and vesicular trafficking regulators, all of which influence mucin production and epithelial homeostasis [2] [3] [4] [5]. Additional evidence shows that reduced goblet cell number is accompanied by thinner mucus, impaired tight junction expression, microbiota disruption, and increased susceptibility to inflammatory injury, whereas interventions that restore goblet cells or mucus can improve barrier integrity and inflammatory status [6] [7] [8]. Thus, this pathway captures a colon barrier-deficient and inflammation-prone state centered on goblet cell depletion.

Building on this intestinal framework, the possible relevance to heart failure can be considered through the broader gut-heart axis, while remaining appropriately cautious because the cited studies do not directly examine heart failure. Although the cited studies do not examine heart failure directly, they provide a biologically plausible framework linking low colonic goblet cell abundance to heart failure through the intestinal barrier, microbial translocation, and systemic inflammation. Heart failure is increasingly understood as a condition accompanied by intestinal hypoperfusion, venous congestion, mucosal dysfunction, and chronic inflammatory activation; within this context, a pre-existing or acquired reduction in colonic goblet cells would be expected to further weaken the mucus barrier and increase host exposure to luminal microbes and microbial products. The strongest mechanistic support from the provided literature is that goblet cell loss disrupts the colonic inner mucus layer and permits bacterial translocation, with concomitant increases in circulating inflammatory mediators such as interleukin 6 and interferon gamma, while goblet cell preservation reverses these effects [1]. This is highly relevant to heart failure because systemic inflammation is a central amplifier of adverse cardiac remodeling, endothelial dysfunction, and multiorgan injury. Complementary studies show that goblet cell deficiency coexists with reduced mucin 2 production, impaired epithelial cytoprotection, lower trefoil factor positive goblet cell populations, altered tight junction proteins, and dysbiotic microbial communities, all of which would be expected to enhance gut-derived inflammatory burden in heart failure [3] [5] [6] [7] [8]. Moreover, pathways that alter goblet cell differentiation, such as Notch inhibition and bone morphogenetic protein signaling, demonstrate that goblet cell abundance is dynamically regulated and tightly coupled to epithelial homeostasis rather than being a passive histologic feature [2] [4]. Taken together, the pathway of low colonic goblet cell abundance is best interpreted in heart failure as a potential contributor to the gut-heart axis: diminished goblet cell mediated mucus defense may promote intestinal permeability, microbial translocation, and persistent inflammatory signaling, which in turn could worsen the systemic inflammatory milieu that drives heart failure progression.[1] [2] [3] [4] [5] [6] [7] [8]

Colon; endocrine cells[≥Low] (HPA:0131291)

The pathway denoted as colon endocrine cells refers to the specialized hormone-producing epithelial cells distributed within the large intestine, including serotonin-producing enterochromaffin cells and peptide-secreting lineages such as glucagon-like peptide one and peptide tyrosine tyrosine producing cells. Although these cells are relatively sparse, they function as an important sensory and endocrine interface between luminal nutrients, microbial metabolites, epithelial metabolism, enteric neural circuits, and systemic physiology. Recent work has shown that differentiation of human intestinal endocrine lineages is tightly controlled by transcriptional programs, with zinc finger protein eight hundred acting as a major repressor of endocrine lineage commitment and particularly limiting enterochromaffin cell differentiation through regulation of an endocrine transcription factor network centered on paired box four, indicating that endocrine cell abundance is actively constrained rather than passively determined [29]. Functionally, colonic endocrine cells detect acute and chronic changes in nutrient availability. Serotonin-secreting enterochromaffin cells release serotonin in response to glucose and also increase tryptophan hydroxylase one transcription and serotonin output during prolonged low-glucose exposure, demonstrating both rapid secretory and slower adaptive transcriptional responses [30]. In parallel, colonic glucagon-like peptide one and peptide tyrosine tyrosine secretion can be stimulated by short-chain fatty acids, especially acetate and butyrate, linking microbial fermentation and colonocyte energy metabolism to endocrine output [31]. Studies of disease states further indicate that altered densities of serotonin and peptide tyrosine tyrosine cells in the colon are associated with functional bowel disorders, supporting the concept that changes in endocrine cell number or activity can reshape motility, secretion, sensory signaling, and broader host physiology [32] [33]. Collectively, this pathway represents a low-abundance but biologically influential colonic endocrine system with potential effects that extend beyond the intestine to whole-body metabolic, inflammatory, and neurohumoral regulation.

With this biological context in mind, the potential relationship between this pathway and heart failure appears to be indirect and should be interpreted cautiously.

The provided literature does not establish a direct causal pathway from colon endocrine cells to heart failure, but it strongly supports a biologically plausible indirect connection through systemic metabolic, inflammatory, neurohumoral, and gut-derived signaling mechanisms that are highly relevant to heart failure pathogenesis and progression. Colonic endocrine cells secrete mediators with recognized extraintestinal actions, most notably serotonin, glucagon-like peptide one, and peptide tyrosine tyrosine. Serotonin-producing enterochromaffin cells in the colon respond dynamically to glucose availability, with acute glucose elevations triggering serotonin release and chronic low-glucose conditions increasing serotonin biosynthetic capacity through upregulation of tryptophan hydroxylase one [30]. Because circulating serotonin can influence vascular tone, platelet biology, autonomic signaling, and fibrotic responses, dysregulated enterochromaffin activity could contribute to the hemodynamic and remodeling milieu characteristic of heart failure, even though this specific cardiac link was not directly tested in the cited studies. Likewise, the colon contains endocrine cells that release glucagon-like peptide one and peptide tyrosine tyrosine in response to short-chain fatty acids such as acetate and butyrate, thereby coupling microbial fermentation and epithelial energy metabolism to endocrine signaling [31]. This observation is relevant to heart failure because heart failure is increasingly understood as a systemic disorder involving gut dysbiosis, impaired intestinal perfusion, metabolic disturbance, and chronic inflammation; therefore, altered production of short-chain fatty acids or impaired responsiveness of colonic endocrine cells could modify glucagon-like peptide one and peptide tyrosine tyrosine signaling, with downstream effects on insulin sensitivity, appetite regulation, body weight, and inflammatory tone, all of which are central determinants of heart failure severity. The developmental study identifying zinc finger protein eight hundred as a master repressor of enteroendocrine differentiation further suggests that endocrine cell abundance and subtype composition are actively regulated and may be remodeled under disease conditions, creating a mechanistic framework by which chronic systemic stress states could alter the endocrine output of the colon [29]. Additional support for a heart failure-relevant interpretation comes from the broader literature on adipokines and adiponectin, which emphasizes that endocrine mediators governing inflammation, metabolism, and organ cross-talk influence the heart as well as the colon, and that adiponectin exerts anti-inflammatory and cardioprotective actions while also protecting the colon, highlighting shared systemic regulatory networks rather than isolated organ biology [34] [35]. Finally, studies showing reduced densities of serotonin and peptide tyrosine tyrosine cells in colonic disorders indicate that the colonic endocrine compartment is sensitive to pathological remodeling and that changes in this compartment can affect motility, secretion, and serotonin signaling [32] [33]. Taken together, the most defensible conclusion is that colon endocrine cells are related to heart failure indirectly, by participating in the gut-heart axis through regulation of serotonin-dependent neurohumoral signaling, short-chain-fatty-acid-responsive glucagon-like peptide one and peptide tyrosine tyrosine secretion, and broader metabolic and inflammatory homeostasis that shapes susceptibility to and progression of heart failure.[29] [30] [31] [32] [33] [34] [35]

Intracranial hemorrhage refers to bleeding within the cranial vault, including bleeding into the brain parenchyma, ventricular system, subarachnoid space, subdural space, or epidural space. Amyloidosis (HP:0011034)

Amyloidosis is a pathological process defined by the extracellular deposition of insoluble misfolded protein fibrils within tissues and organs, leading to progressive structural distortion and organ dysfunction. In the cardiovascular setting, the most clinically important forms are transthyretin amyloidosis and immunoglobulin light-chain amyloidosis. Transthyretin amyloidosis arises when transthyretin, a liver-derived transport protein, becomes unstable, misfolds, and aggregates into amyloid fibrils; this may occur either because of inherited sequence variants or through age-related misfolding of the normal protein. Immunoglobulin light-chain amyloidosis results from monoclonal plasma cell production of unstable light chains that misfold and deposit systemically. In the heart, amyloid deposition occurs predominantly in the myocardial interstitium, but may also involve the conduction system, valves, and small vessels. This infiltration increases ventricular wall thickness, reduces ventricular compliance, impairs diastolic filling, and later compromises systolic performance, thereby producing a restrictive cardiomyopathic phenotype. Contemporary clinical literature emphasizes that cardiac amyloidosis is no longer viewed as a rare curiosity, but rather as an increasingly recognized cause of heart failure, particularly among older adults and among patients with heart failure with preserved ejection fraction. Improved noninvasive diagnostic strategies, especially radionuclide scintigraphy combined with testing to exclude immunoglobulin light-chain disease, have substantially increased recognition of transthyretin-related cardiac disease and enabled earlier therapeutic intervention. Importantly, this pathway is directly actionable, because transthyretin silencers, transthyretin stabilizers, and plasma cell-directed therapies have demonstrated that reducing precursor protein supply or suppressing the amyloidogenic clone can slow disease progression, preserve functional capacity, improve quality of life, reduce cardiovascular events, and in some settings improve survival.

Against this pathological background, the relationship between amyloidosis and heart failure appears direct, causal, and increasingly well established. Cardiac amyloid deposition transforms the myocardium into a stiff, noncompliant, infiltrated ventricle, producing elevated filling pressures, impaired relaxation, reduced exercise tolerance, congestion, arrhythmia susceptibility, and progressive heart failure; in advanced disease, mortality is driven largely by amyloid cardiomyopathy. Transthyretin cardiac amyloidosis is now recognized as an important and underdiagnosed cause of heart failure worldwide, including a substantial proportion of patients hospitalized with heart failure with preserved ejection fraction, and delayed diagnosis has historically contributed to poor outcomes and prolonged healthcare use before recognition of the disease. The clinical importance of this pathway in heart failure is further illustrated by interventional studies showing that modifying amyloid biology improves cardiac outcomes. In patients with transthyretin amyloidosis with cardiomyopathy, vutrisiran reduced the combined risk of death and recurrent cardiovascular events, lowered all-cause mortality, and attenuated decline in walking capacity and cardiomyopathy-related quality of life, directly indicating that suppression of transthyretin production favorably alters the heart failure trajectory. Similarly, patisiran preserved functional capacity and improved health status in transthyretin cardiac amyloidosis, supporting the concept that reduction of amyloid precursor supply can mitigate cardiac functional deterioration. Earlier studies of patisiran and inotersen in hereditary transthyretin amyloidosis with polyneuropathy also showed benefit regardless of the presence of cardiomyopathy, reinforcing the systemic relevance of transthyretin suppression. The mechanistic rationale is strengthened by in vivo gene editing directed against transthyretin, which produced marked reductions in circulating transthyretin concentrations, providing proof that durable upstream interruption of this amyloidogenic pathway is feasible. Heart failure relevance is not limited to transthyretin disease: in immunoglobulin light-chain amyloidosis, daratumumab-based therapy improved hematologic response and increased cardiac organ responses, while deaths were still frequently attributable to amyloidosis-related cardiomyopathy, underscoring the central role of cardiac involvement in prognosis. Collectively, these studies suggest that amyloidosis is not merely associated with heart failure but may represent a major infiltrative pathway that causes restrictive cardiomyopathy and progressive heart failure, and that therapies targeting amyloid formation or precursor production can meaningfully improve cardiac morbidity and survival.[92] [93] [94] [95] [96] [97] [98] [99] [100] [101] [102] [103] [104]

Neutrophil degranulation (REAC:R-HSA-6798695)

Neutrophil degranulation is a core effector process of innate immunity in which activated neutrophils mobilize and release the contents of intracellular granules into phagosomes or into the extracellular space. It directly links oxidized lipid uptake to endothelial dysfunction, oxidative stress, inflammation, and atherogenesis, all of which are major antecedents of ischemic heart disease and heart failure. Through nuclear factor kappa B activation, reduced nitric oxide release, and promotion of leukocyte adhesion and apoptosis, OLR1 contributes to vascular injury and impaired perfusion. These mechanisms are highly relevant not only to coronary artery disease leading to heart failure, but also to microvascular dysfunction and systemic vascular inflammation that can worsen cardiac loading conditions and myocardial remodeling. Its additional recognition of advanced glycation end products, apoptotic cells, and bacteria further places OLR1 at the intersection of metabolic stress, inflammation, and vascular pathology.

A somewhat more indirect but still relevant cardiometabolic connection may be considered for PTPRN2.

PTPRN2 is potentially relevant to heart failure primarily through its role in insulin secretion and phosphoinositide-regulated membrane dynamics. Diabetes and insulin resistance are major drivers of heart-failure risk and progression, and proteins that govern insulin vesicle stability and secretion may therefore influence systemic metabolic stress imposed on the myocardium. Beyond endocrine effects, its regulation of phosphatidylinositol four, five bisphosphate and actin dynamics suggests broader relevance to vesicular trafficking and cytoskeletal remodeling, processes that can affect secretory and signaling functions in multiple cell types. Although the connection to heart failure is more indirect than for inflammatory or vascular proteins, PTPRN2 may be important in the cardiometabolic dimension of disease.[40] [41] [42] [43] [44] [45]

Immune sensing remains a recurring theme across this protein set, and RNASET2 adds a potentially important connection to nucleic acid handling and mitochondrial stress responses.

RNASET2 is notable because it connects nucleic-acid sensing, innate immune activation, and mitochondrial ribonucleic acid handling, all of which are increasingly implicated in inflammatory cardiac injury. Toll like receptor seven and toll like receptor eight mediated immune activation can contribute to antiviral responses but may also exacerbate myocardial inflammation during viral infection or sterile tissue damage. In addition, mitochondrial nucleic acid metabolism is highly relevant to cardiomyocyte stress responses, given the centrality of mitochondrial dysfunction in heart failure. Thus, RNASET2 may be mechanistically linked to myocarditis, inflammatory remodeling, and mitochondrial stress signaling in the failing heart.[245] [246] [247] [248] [249]

Calcium-linked inflammatory signaling offers another possible angle, making S100P a protein of potential interest in this context.

S100P is of interest in heart failure because calcium signaling and receptor for advanced glycation end products mediated inflammatory pathways are both highly relevant to cardiovascular disease. Dysregulated calcium handling is a hallmark of failing cardiomyocytes, while activation of receptor for advanced glycation end products by its ligands contributes to inflammation, fibrosis, oxidative stress, and diabetic cardiovascular complications. Although S100P is less directly established in heart failure than canonical cardiovascular mediators, its ability to engage receptor for advanced glycation end products suggests a plausible role in amplifying inflammatory and profibrotic signaling, particularly in metabolic disease-associated heart failure.

At the level of cellular stress adaptation, TXNDC5 may be relevant because protein folding stress and redox imbalance are frequently implicated in myocardial dysfunction.

TXNDC5 is relevant to heart failure because endoplasmic reticulum stress, oxidative protein folding, and proteostasis failure are important contributors to cardiomyocyte dysfunction and maladaptive remodeling. As a protein disulfide isomerase, TXNDC5 may influence the folding and maturation of secreted and membrane proteins under stress conditions, thereby modulating cellular adaptation to pressure overload, ischemia, or inflammation. Its relation to insulin chemistry further suggests possible links to metabolic disease, which is tightly intertwined with heart-failure pathogenesis. Thus, TXNDC5 may be important at the interface of endoplasmic reticulum stress, redox imbalance, and cardiometabolic dysfunction.[250] [251]

Neurohumoral regulation represents an additional dimension of heart failure biology, and ADA2 may be considered within that framework.

ADA2 is relevant to heart failure because chronic sympathetic activation is a defining feature of the syndrome. Alpha two adrenergic signaling modulates catecholamine responses and cyclic adenosine monophosphate generation, processes that influence vascular tone, neurohumoral activation, and cardiac workload. Although this protein is not as directly tied to structural remodeling as some others in the list, altered adrenergic signaling is central to heart-failure progression and therapeutic response. Therefore, ADA2 may contribute to the neurohormonal dysregulation characteristic of heart failure.

Finally, balancing the pro-inflammatory candidates above, LAIR1 may represent a counter-regulatory component within the same broader immune landscape.

LAIR1 is potentially important in heart failure because it represents an endogenous brake on immune activation. Excessive immune-cell activation and cytokine production contribute to chronic inflammation, fibrosis, and ventricular remodeling in heart failure. By suppressing nuclear factor kappa B signaling and inflammatory cytokine output while promoting more tolerogenic immune responses, LAIR1 may counterbalance the inflammatory processes that worsen myocardial injury. This makes it a plausible immunoregulatory factor in inflammatory or autoimmune forms of cardiac dysfunction, even if the connection is more modulatory than primary.

Pleural mesothelioma (WP:WP5087)

Pleural mesothelioma is a malignant disease arising from mesothelial cells of the pleura, the serosal membrane surrounding the lungs and lining the thoracic cavity. It is classically associated with prior asbestos exposure and is characterized by long latency, diffuse pleural growth, progressive encasement of the lung, intense local inflammation, stromal remodeling, and marked molecular heterogeneity. Contemporary studies indicate that pleural mesothelioma is not a single biologic entity but rather a spectrum of tumors defined by differences in morphology, genomic instability, immune response, methylation state, and recurrent alterations involving genes such as BRCA1 associated protein 1, neurofibromin 2, and cyclin dependent kinase inhibitor 2A. Therapeutically, management includes surgery in highly selected patients, platinum and pemetrexed based chemotherapy, immune checkpoint blockade, antiangiogenic therapy, and emerging biomarker guided approaches. Recent randomized and phase two studies have shown clinically meaningful activity for nivolumab plus ipilimumab in unresectable disease, durvalumab combined with platinum and pemetrexed, ramucirumab plus gemcitabine in previously treated disease, pegargiminase with first line chemotherapy in nonepithelioid tumors, and enhancer of zeste homolog 2 inhibition in biomarker selected relapsed disease. At the same time, multimodality local therapy can impose substantial cardiopulmonary stress, and randomized surgical evidence suggests increased serious cardiac and respiratory toxicity with aggressive pleural surgery. Thus, although pleural mesothelioma is primarily a thoracic malignancy rather than a canonical heart failure signaling pathway, it intersects with heart failure through shared inflammatory and vascular biology, direct cardiopulmonary mechanical burden, and treatment related cardiovascular toxicity.

Building on this clinical and biologic context, the relationship between pleural mesothelioma and heart failure appears to be chiefly clinical and pathobiological rather than a direct causal heart failure pathway. First, pleural mesothelioma develops within the thoracic cavity and produces progressive pleural thickening, restrictive lung mechanics, hypoxemia, pulmonary vascular stress, recurrent pleural effusions, and chronic systemic inflammation; together, these processes may increase right ventricular afterload, worsen pre existing cardiac dysfunction, and precipitate decompensation in susceptible patients. Second, several treatments used in pleural mesothelioma have documented cardiovascular consequences that are directly relevant to heart failure. In the phase three trial of nivolumab plus ipilimumab for unresectable malignant pleural mesothelioma, treatment related death from heart failure was reported, demonstrating that immune checkpoint therapy can rarely trigger severe cardiovascular toxicity in this population [252]. In the randomized trial comparing extended pleurectomy decortication plus chemotherapy with chemotherapy alone, the surgical strategy produced substantially more serious adverse events, including a higher incidence of cardiac disorders, indicating that aggressive resection can impose major hemodynamic and cardiopulmonary stress that may contribute to heart failure risk [253]. The surgery after radiotherapy protocol also showed frequent severe perioperative morbidity after hemithoracic radiotherapy followed by extrapleural pneumonectomy, further supporting the concept that intensive local therapy can challenge cardiovascular reserve [254]. Antiangiogenic treatment provides another mechanistic link: ramucirumab improved survival in pretreated pleural mesothelioma but increased severe hypertension and thromboembolic toxicity, both established contributors to cardiac dysfunction and heart failure progression [255]. More broadly, multiomic studies show that pleural mesothelioma is shaped by immune activation, stromal remodeling, and interpatient biologic heterogeneity, while spatial profiling demonstrates marked variation in extracellular matrix pathways and immune mediator expression across tumor sites; these features are relevant because inflammatory cytokine signaling, fibrosis, and vascular remodeling are central drivers of adverse cardiac remodeling in heart failure, suggesting overlapping disease biology even if the primary disease is malignant rather than cardiac [256] [257]. Therefore, pleural mesothelioma is related to heart failure through thoracic mechanical burden, inflammation associated cardiopulmonary compromise, and especially through treatment associated cardiac toxicity, making cardiovascular assessment and surveillance important in affected patients.[252] [253] [254] [255] [256] [257]

Extracellular matrix organization (REAC:R-HSA-1474244)

Extracellular matrix organization is the coordinated process by which structural proteins, adhesive glycoproteins, proteoglycans, matrix-modifying enzymes, and matricellular regulators are synthesized, assembled, cross-linked, aligned, remodeled, and degraded to generate a functional tissue scaffold. This pathway influences not only tissue architecture and mechanical strength, but also cell adhesion, migration, differentiation, survival, and signal transduction, because the extracellular matrix serves as both a biomechanical framework and a reservoir for growth factors and cytokines. The publications provided collectively highlight several central features of this pathway. During cardiac development, proper extracellular matrix organization is required for epicardial progenitor migration and morphogenesis of the atrial ventricular canal, suggesting that cardiac cells depend on tightly regulated matrix composition and spatial patterning for normal heart formation [265]. Mechanical cues also appear to be major determinants of extracellular matrix organization, as force-dependent transforming growth factor beta signaling regulates matrix production and cellular morphology in connective tissues [266]. In ageing cardiac tissue, extracellular matrix remodeling involves both biochemical composition and tissue stiffness, and these matrix properties directly influence cardiac fibroblast activation, senescence, and profibrotic behavior [267]. Additional studies indicate that extracellular matrix organization is shaped by pathways involving integrins, matrix metallopeptidases, collagen cross-linking enzymes, and adhesion regulators, all of which influence how cells sense and respond to their microenvironment [268] [269] [270]. Taken together, this pathway may be viewed as a dynamic and tightly regulated program that integrates structural remodeling, mechanotransduction, and cell signaling, and is therefore likely to be important for tissue homeostasis, repair, ageing, and chronic disease.

Against this broader biological background, the potential relevance of extracellular matrix organization to heart failure becomes more coherent. Extracellular matrix organization is highly relevant to heart failure because heart failure is fundamentally a disease of maladaptive cardiac remodeling in which changes in matrix composition, stiffness, alignment, turnover, and cell-matrix signaling progressively impair ventricular structure and function. The most directly informative study among the provided publications shows that in ageing cardiac tissue, extracellular matrix remodeling alters both ligand presentation and mechanical stiffness, and that these two features independently regulate cardiac fibroblast activation, matrix remodeling, and senescence; importantly, a youthful extracellular matrix composition can suppress profibrotic fibroblast behavior even in a stiff environment, demonstrating that matrix organization is not merely a passive consequence of disease but may also act as an active determinant of cardiac cellular state [267]. This observation is highly pertinent to heart failure, where activated cardiac fibroblasts drive interstitial and perivascular fibrosis, increase ventricular stiffness, and disrupt electromechanical coupling. Developmental evidence further supports the cardiac importance of this pathway, as ten-eleven translocation enzyme dependent regulation of extracellular matrix organization is required for epicardial progenitor migration and heart morphogenesis in zebrafish, indicating that the heart is intrinsically dependent on precise matrix patterning for normal tissue assembly and implying that later-life matrix disorganization may also have major functional consequences [265]. Mechanistic studies in other systems reinforce concepts that may be transferable to heart failure pathobiology: mechanical force can induce transforming growth factor beta dependent extracellular matrix production and cellular remodeling [266], integrin-mediated matrix assembly controls downstream signaling environments [268], hypoxia-inducible factor one alpha signaling can couple metabolism to collagen organization and cross-linking through procollagen lysine hydroxylation and lysyl oxidase pathways [269], and impaired endothelial adhesion with defective matrix organization can itself drive cardiopulmonary vascular disease that is reversible with matrix metallopeptidase inhibition [270]. Taken together, these studies support a model in which disturbed extracellular matrix organization may contribute to heart failure by promoting fibroblast activation, collagen deposition and cross-linking, excessive tissue stiffening, abnormal force transmission, endothelial dysfunction, and persistent profibrotic signaling, thereby accelerating adverse cardiac remodeling and functional decline.[265] [266] [267] [268] [269] [270]

This conceptual framework is further supported by the enrichment results obtained in the present analysis. There were 62 heart failure-associated proteins significantly enriched in the pathway of extracellular matrix organization, indicating a substantial convergence of disease-related molecular signals on the regulation of tissue scaffold assembly, remodeling, and degradation. This enrichment is highly consistent with the central role of extracellular matrix dynamics in cardiac structure and function, because extracellular matrix organization governs mechanical strength, cell adhesion, migration, differentiation, survival, and signal transduction through the coordinated control of structural proteins, adhesive glycoproteins, proteoglycans, matrix-modifying enzymes, and matricellular regulators. The biological relevance of this pathway to the heart is strongly supported by evidence showing that proper extracellular matrix organization is required for epicardial progenitor migration and morphogenesis of the atrial ventricular canal during cardiac development [265]. In parallel, force-dependent transforming growth factor beta signaling has been shown to regulate matrix production and cellular morphology, demonstrating that biomechanical stress is quantitatively and functionally integrated into extracellular matrix remodeling programs [266]. In ageing cardiac tissue, extracellular matrix remodeling is further linked to altered biochemical composition and increased stiffness, both of which directly influence cardiac fibroblast activation, senescence, and profibrotic behavior, thereby providing a mechanistic bridge between age-related matrix alterations and progressive cardiac dysfunction [267]. Additional studies indicate that this pathway is shaped by integrin signaling, matrix metallopeptidase activity, collagen cross-linking enzymes, and adhesion regulators, together forming a multicomponent network that controls how cardiac cells sense and respond to their surrounding microenvironment [268] [269] [270]. Taken together, the identification of 62 enriched proteins supports the interpretation that extracellular matrix organization is not a peripheral process in heart failure, but rather a quantitatively prominent and biologically integrated pathway that links structural remodeling, mechanotransduction, ageing-associated stiffness, and maladaptive repair.
