Introduction

Alzheimer's disease (Disease code: alzheimer) is a progressive neurodegenerative disorder marked by cognitive decline, synaptic dysfunction, neuronal loss, and the accumulation of pathogenic protein aggregates in the brain. In a study using data from the United Kingdom Biobank Pharma Proteomics Project, circulating levels of 10 plasma proteins were significantly associated with Alzheimer's disease among 52,344 controls and 676 cases, following Bonferroni correction at a significance threshold of P < 0.000000001. Investigating these proteins is crucial for understanding the molecular mechanisms underlying Alzheimer's disease, particularly given the marked imbalance between cases and controls, and for identifying biological processes that may contribute to disease onset and progression. A comprehensive pathway enrichment analysis was conducted using these 10 proteins, drawing on annotations from Gene Ontology Molecular Function, Gene Ontology Biological Process, Gene Ontology Cellular Component, Reactome, and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes. This analysis revealed 29 significantly enriched biological pathways, emphasizing neuronal and synaptic functions. Key pathways included transmission across chemical synapses, synaptic vesicle pathways, neuronal systems, and receptor-ligand activity, highlighting themes such as synaptic signaling, vesicle trafficking, axonal structure, and receptor-mediated communication. These findings suggest that the disease-associated proteins converge on pathways central to synaptic failure, neuronal connectivity, and intercellular signaling, which are critical features of Alzheimer's disease. The following sections will delve into the most significant pathways and their protein interaction patterns to elucidate their potential contributions to the molecular framework of Alzheimer's disease.

    1. vesicle organization (GO:0016050)

    Agent Summary: Vesicle organization is a critical cellular process involved in the management of membrane-bounded vesicles, essential for synaptic transmission, protein turnover, and intercellular communication. Key proteins: APOE.
    Read Context ▽

    Vesicle organization is a critical cellular process involved in the management of membrane-bounded vesicles, essential for synaptic transmission, protein turnover, and intercellular communication. In the context of Alzheimer's disease, disruptions in vesicle organization are linked to synaptic failure, amyloid and tau pathology, and neuroinflammation. Studies have shown that Alzheimer's disease is associated with altered presynaptic vesicle architecture, such as reduced vesicle density and abnormal vesicle enlargement, which correlate with synaptic loss and cognitive decline. Both beta amyloid and tau proteinopathies have been found to directly disturb vesicle arrangement at synapses, further implicating vesicle dysregulation in disease mechanisms. Additionally, extracellular vesicles are implicated in Alzheimer's pathogenesis, as they are found within beta amyloid plaques and are involved in plaque-associated protein networks, suggesting their role in the disease's progression.

    2. intermediate filament (GO:0005882)

    Agent Summary: The intermediate filament pathway is implicated in Alzheimer's disease through its roles in both neuronal and glial contexts. Key proteins: APOE, GFAP.
    Read Context ▽

    The intermediate filament pathway is implicated in Alzheimer's disease through its roles in both neuronal and glial contexts. Neuronal intermediate filaments, particularly neurofilament light chain, are elevated in Alzheimer's patients and serve as markers of axonal degeneration, correlating with cognitive decline and neurodegeneration. This elevation reflects ongoing axonal injury rather than being a direct cause of the disease. On the glial side, astrocytic intermediate filaments, especially glial fibrillary acidic protein, are involved in the early stages of Alzheimer's pathogenesis, mediating amyloid-related processes and contributing to synaptic and network dysfunction. Experimental studies suggest that targeting astrocytic intermediate filament remodeling could potentially ameliorate cognitive deficits in Alzheimer's models, highlighting its pathogenic role.

    The cell lineage map for neuronal differentiation outlines the progression of stem cells and progenitor cells into mature neurons, integrating intrinsic transcription factors and extracellular signals. This pathway is crucial in understanding Alzheimer disease, as it affects not only mature neurons but also the cellular programs responsible for neuronal lineage generation and maintenance. Studies using induced pluripotent stem cell-derived models have shown that Alzheimer-like pathology can emerge during neuronal differentiation, with enhanced wingless-related integration site signaling reducing amyloid and tau pathology. Aging studies indicate that neuronal lineages are particularly vulnerable to Alzheimer disease, with increased expression of amyloid precursor protein and tau in aged neurons. Experimental evidence suggests that manipulating regulators of lineage progression, such as forkhead box G1, may improve neuronal differentiation and counteract Alzheimer-related deficits.

    4. chemical synaptic transmission (GO:0007268)

    Chemical synaptic transmission is a crucial pathway for neuron communication, involving the release and reception of neurotransmitters across synapses. In the context of Alzheimer disease, disruptions in this pathway are not just secondary effects but central to the disease's progression, linking molecular pathology to cognitive decline. Studies have shown that genes associated with synaptic transmission are enriched in Alzheimer-related brain abnormalities, suggesting that synaptic dysfunction is a key feature of the disease. Tau pathology and amyloid beta production are implicated in disrupting synaptic processes, reinforcing the idea that Alzheimer disease is fundamentally a synaptic disorder. Additionally, research highlights the role of dysregulated genes and non-coding RNAs in affecting synaptic transmission, further emphasizing its importance in Alzheimer disease biology.

    5. receptor ligand activity (GO:0048018)

    Agent Summary: Receptor ligand activity is a critical mechanism in the brain, facilitating communication between various cell types, including neurons, glia, and endothelial cells, through ligand-receptor interactions. Key proteins: APOE, GDF15.

    Receptor ligand activity is a critical mechanism in the brain, facilitating communication between various cell types, including neurons, glia, and endothelial cells, through ligand-receptor interactions. In Alzheimer's disease, disruptions in this pathway contribute to disease progression by affecting neurovascular communication, glia-neuron signaling, and immune checkpoint pathways. For instance, alterations in ephrin type-A receptor 1 and its signaling can lead to impaired blood-brain barrier function and increased inflammation. Additionally, changes in ligand-receptor communication involving amyloid beta precursor protein and apolipoprotein E can disrupt neuronal signaling pathways, contributing to neuronal vulnerability and cognitive decline. Therapeutic strategies targeting these receptor-ligand interactions, such as modulating microglial inhibitory receptors or immune checkpoint pathways, may offer potential benefits in mitigating Alzheimer's disease pathology.

    The synaptic vesicle pathway involves the intricate processes that manage the lifecycle of synaptic vesicles, including their formation, loading with neurotransmitters, and eventual release into the synaptic cleft, which is crucial for effective neurotransmission. This pathway's proper functioning is vital for maintaining synaptic plasticity and circuit integrity, and disruptions can lead to impaired synaptic function even before neuronal death occurs. In Alzheimer's disease, the synaptic vesicle pathway is critically relevant as synaptic dysfunction is an early indicator of cognitive decline. Additionally, this pathway is interconnected with key Alzheimer-related processes such as amyloid precursor protein processing, endocytic trafficking, and inflammatory signaling, linking it to the broader mechanisms of protein aggregation and neurodegeneration. Understanding these connections highlights the pathway's role in the disease's progression and potential therapeutic targets.

    7. lipoprotein particle receptor binding (GO:0070325)

    Agent Summary: The lipoprotein particle receptor binding pathway is crucial in the nervous system for the transport of cholesterol and other lipids, which are essential for maintaining lipid homeostasis, membrane turnover, and synaptic function. Key proteins: APOE, SYT1.
    Read Context ▽

    The lipoprotein particle receptor binding pathway is crucial in the nervous system for the transport of cholesterol and other lipids, which are essential for maintaining lipid homeostasis, membrane turnover, and synaptic function. This process involves the interaction of apolipoproteins, such as apolipoprotein E, with receptors like low-density lipoprotein receptor-related protein 1, which facilitates the clearance of amyloid beta and other waste products. In Alzheimer's disease, disruptions in this pathway can lead to impaired clearance of amyloid beta and tau pathology, contributing to neurodegeneration. The apolipoprotein E4 isoform is a significant genetic risk factor for Alzheimer's, while apolipoprotein E2 offers protection, highlighting the importance of receptor binding dynamics in disease progression. Overall, the pathway represents a critical intersection of lipid metabolism, protein aggregation, and immune responses in Alzheimer's disease.

    8. Neuronal System (REAC:R-HSA-112316)

    Agent Summary: The neuronal system pathway plays a crucial role in Alzheimer's disease by encompassing the cellular and molecular processes that facilitate neuronal communication and support from glial cells.
    Read Context ▽

    The neuronal system pathway plays a crucial role in Alzheimer's disease by encompassing the cellular and molecular processes that facilitate neuronal communication and support from glial cells. In Alzheimer's, this pathway is disrupted, leading to a failure in neuronal signaling, synaptic maintenance, and circuit homeostasis, which is exacerbated by pathological interactions with glial and immune cells. Studies using human three-dimensional culture models and single-nucleus analysis of the human cortex have shown that neurons, astrocytes, and microglia collectively contribute to amyloid beta aggregation, tau pathology, and neuroinflammatory signaling, indicating that these processes are intertwined with neuronal dysfunction. Mechanistic studies highlight that alterations in intracellular trafficking and synaptic biology, driven by specific proteins and genetic risk factors, lead to synaptic and memory impairments, suggesting that synaptic homeostasis is a core vulnerability in Alzheimer's disease. At the circuit level, amyloid beta is linked to a pattern of neuronal hyperactivity and silencing, further implicating disrupted neuronal activity in the disease's progression.

    9. Transmission across Chemical Synapses (REAC:R-HSA-112315)

    Agent Summary: Transmission across chemical synapses is crucial for neuron-to-neuron communication, involving neurotransmitter release, receptor activation, and synaptic plasticity, which are essential for learning and memory.
    Read Context ▽

    Transmission across chemical synapses is crucial for neuron-to-neuron communication, involving neurotransmitter release, receptor activation, and synaptic plasticity, which are essential for learning and memory. In Alzheimer disease, synaptic dysfunction is an early event that precedes significant neuronal death and may be influenced by various pathogenic factors such as amyloid beta, tau pathology, and neuroinflammation. Studies indicate that synapses in Alzheimer models show defects in long-term potentiation, a key process for synaptic strengthening, with impaired receptor insertion and disrupted signaling pathways. Both excitatory and inhibitory synaptic transmissions are affected, with evidence of reduced gamma aminobutyric acid (GABA) transmission leading to network hyperexcitability and cognitive decline. These findings suggest that synaptic transmission is a central site of molecular pathology in Alzheimer disease, potentially offering targets for therapeutic intervention.

    10. axon (GO:0030424)

    Agent Summary: The axon is a critical component in neurons for transmitting electrical impulses, and its function is highly dependent on various cellular processes such as intracellular transport and membrane integrity. Key proteins: APOE, GDF15, GFAP.
    Read Context ▽

    The axon is a critical component in neurons for transmitting electrical impulses, and its function is highly dependent on various cellular processes such as intracellular transport and membrane integrity. In Alzheimer's disease, axons are not just passive victims of plaque and tau pathology but are active sites of dysfunction, characterized by dystrophic swellings and impaired conduction. Axonal pathology is central to Alzheimer's pathogenesis, with plaque-associated axonal spheroids disrupting neural connectivity and network activity. These disruptions are linked to endolysosomal accumulation and can be mitigated by targeting specific molecular pathways, highlighting the role of axonal trafficking failures in disease progression. Furthermore, early axonal damage, such as the loss of noradrenergic axons in the locus coeruleus, occurs before plaque deposition and contributes to early cognitive dysfunction, underscoring the axon's pivotal role in Alzheimer's disease.
