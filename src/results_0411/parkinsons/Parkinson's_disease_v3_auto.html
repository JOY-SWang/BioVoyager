Introduction

Parkinson's disease (Disease code: PARKINSONS) is a progressive neurodegenerative disorder marked by dopaminergic neuronal loss, abnormal protein aggregation, and both motor and non-motor dysfunction. In a study using the UK Biobank Pharma Proteomics Project data, 19 plasma proteins were significantly associated with Parkinson's disease, based on a cohort of 52,100 controls and 874 cases, with significance determined by a Bonferroni-corrected threshold of P < 0.000000001. Investigating these proteins is crucial for understanding systemic molecular signals that reflect neuronal injury, neuroimmune activation, and tissue remodeling in Parkinson's disease. To explore these mechanisms, we conducted a functional enrichment analysis using Gene Ontology Molecular Function, Gene Ontology Biological Process, Gene Ontology Cellular Component, Reactome, and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes databases. This analysis revealed 59 significantly enriched pathways, highlighting key biological themes such as extracellular matrix organization, integrin-mediated adhesion, cell-surface signaling, phagocytosis, microglial immune activity, and broader stress-response and neuroendocrine processes. These findings suggest that Parkinson's disease involves not only neuron-intrinsic degeneration but also altered neuron-microenvironment interactions, chronic inflammatory clearance mechanisms, adhesion-dependent signaling, and systemic neuroendocrine components. The following sections will delve into the most significant pathways and their associated protein interaction patterns to further elucidate the molecular framework captured by these disease-associated plasma proteins.

    1. Pituitary gland (HPA:0370000)

    Agent Summary: The pituitary gland plays a role in Parkinson's disease through neuroendocrine regulation, protein aggregation, and clinical complications related to pituitary disease or its treatment. Key proteins: PTPRN2, SCG2, VGF.
    Read Context ▽

    The pituitary gland plays a role in Parkinson's disease through neuroendocrine regulation, protein aggregation, and clinical complications related to pituitary disease or its treatment. It contributes to systemic hormonal environments that may affect brain vulnerability, with disturbances in growth hormone and insulin-like growth factor signaling potentially representing risk factors for neurodegenerative disorders, including Parkinson's. Pathological alpha-synuclein deposits in the pituitary suggest its involvement in Parkinson-related proteinopathy, although there is no evidence of disease transmission through pituitary-derived material. Additionally, pituitary pathology can mimic Parkinson's symptoms, as seen in cases of parkinsonism following pituitary surgery. Overall, while the pituitary gland is not central to Parkinson's disease, it intersects with the disease's biology through these mechanisms.

    2. Adrenal gland; medullary cells[≥Low] (HPA:0020061)

    Agent Summary: The adrenal gland medullary cells, or chromaffin cells, are neuroendocrine cells derived from the neural crest and are involved in catecholamine production, playing a significant role in stress response. Key proteins: PTPRN2, SCG2, VGF.
    Read Context ▽

    The adrenal gland medullary cells, or chromaffin cells, are neuroendocrine cells derived from the neural crest and are involved in catecholamine production, playing a significant role in stress response. These cells exhibit developmental and functional heterogeneity and are integrated into broader endocrine circuits, synthesizing catecholamines through specific enzymes regulated by stress-related pathways. In the context of Parkinson's disease, these cells are of interest due to their biological similarities with dopaminergic systems, and experimental studies have shown that transplantation of adrenal medullary tissue can influence dopaminergic function and provide neurotrophic support. This support is not through direct neurotransmitter replacement but rather through promoting striatal reinnervation and secreting neurotrophic factors, which may help alleviate motor deficits in Parkinson's models. Additionally, the involvement of adrenal medullary cells in stress-responsive signaling pathways may further link them to Parkinson's disease pathogenesis through their impact on neuroimmune states.

    3. Focal adhesion (KEGG:04510)

    Agent Summary: Focal adhesion is a crucial cell signaling and structural pathway that connects the extracellular matrix to the intracellular actin cytoskeleton, involving integrin receptors and a complex of proteins like focal adhesion kinase (FAK), paxillin, and vinculin.
    Read Context ▽

    Focal adhesion is a crucial cell signaling and structural pathway that connects the extracellular matrix to the intracellular actin cytoskeleton, involving integrin receptors and a complex of proteins like focal adhesion kinase (FAK), paxillin, and vinculin. This pathway is integral for mechanotransduction, enabling cells to sense and respond to mechanical cues from their environment, which influences processes such as adhesion, migration, and survival. In the context of Parkinson's disease, the Gly2019Ser mutation in leucine-rich repeat kinase 2 (LRRK2) has been shown to inhibit FAK signaling, impairing microglial motility and their response to brain injury, which could contribute to neurodegenerative progression by weakening protective glial functions. The pathway's regulation of mechanotransduction, adhesion turnover, and calcium signaling is critical for how microglia and other neural cells respond to tissue damage in the diseased brain. Overall, focal adhesion serves as a central hub for translating extracellular mechanical signals into intracellular responses, with implications for neurodegenerative diseases like Parkinson's.

    4. Extracellular matrix organization (REAC:R-HSA-1474244)

    Agent Summary: Extracellular matrix organization plays a crucial role in the structural and functional integrity of tissues by regulating cell behavior, adhesion, and communication.
    Read Context ▽

    Extracellular matrix organization plays a crucial role in the structural and functional integrity of tissues by regulating cell behavior, adhesion, and communication. In the context of Parkinson's disease, alterations in the extracellular matrix, particularly due to synucleinopathy, can influence the vulnerability of dopaminergic neurons, neuroinflammation, and the spread of pathological alpha-synuclein. Experimental evidence from mouse models shows that changes in the extracellular matrix, such as hyaluronan degradation, are not merely secondary effects but actively contribute to disease progression. The dysregulation of extracellular matrix components, including glycoproteins like reelin, is observed across various neurodegenerative diseases, indicating a broader relevance beyond Parkinson's disease. These findings suggest that targeting extracellular matrix organization could be a potential therapeutic strategy to modify disease severity and progression in Parkinson's disease.

    5. opsonin binding (GO:0001846)

    Read Context ▽

    Opsonin binding involves the recognition of opsonins, such as antibodies or complement fragments, by receptors on phagocytes, facilitating the clearance of microbes and cellular debris. This process is crucial for both innate and adaptive immunity, as it triggers phagocyte responses like engulfment and inflammatory signaling. In the context of Parkinson's disease, while opsonin binding is not a primary pathway, it is relevant due to its role in neuroinflammation and the clearance of damaged cells and protein aggregates, such as misfolded alpha-synuclein. The disease is characterized by sustained innate immune activation in the brain, with glial cells playing a dynamic role in disease progression. Complement-opsonin interactions may contribute to the inflammatory clearance of neuronal debris, highlighting the potential involvement of opsonin binding in Parkinson's disease pathology.

    Read Context ▽

    Cell-matrix adhesion is a biological process that connects cells to the extracellular matrix through integrin-centered assemblies, linking extracellular signals to intracellular responses. This pathway is crucial for various cellular functions, including shape, migration, and survival, by integrating extracellular matrix binding with cytoskeletal organization and mechanosensation. In the context of Parkinson's disease, disruptions in cell-matrix adhesion may contribute to synaptic dysfunction by affecting actin- and microtubule-dependent processes essential for synaptic vesicle cycling. This connection is supported by evidence showing that cell-matrix adhesion is vital for organizing the actin cytoskeleton and force transmission, which are critical for neurotransmitter release. Additionally, phosphoproteomic analyses in Parkinson's patients reveal altered cell-matrix adhesion processes, suggesting a systemic alteration of this pathway in the disease.

    The Hippo Merlin signaling pathway involves a network centered on the protein Merlin, which regulates the activity of the yes-associated protein 1 (YAP1) and tafazzin through the Hippo kinase cascade, thereby controlling gene transcription related to cell proliferation and survival. Dysregulation of this pathway, often due to loss or impairment of Merlin, leads to increased activity of YAP1 and tafazzin, resulting in pathological transcriptional programs that can promote tumor growth and affect cell differentiation and regeneration. In the context of Parkinson's disease, while direct mechanistic studies are lacking, the pathway's involvement in neuroinflammation and its regulation of cell-state transitions and stress responses suggest a potential role in the disease's pathology, particularly concerning glial dysfunction and neuroinflammation. The pathway's influence on inflammatory mediators and its response to environmental stress further highlight its relevance to neurodegenerative processes, including those seen in Parkinson's disease.

    8. phagocytosis (GO:0006909)

    Agent Summary: Phagocytosis in the context of Parkinson's disease involves the microglial clearance of alpha-synuclein aggregates, myelin debris, and inflammatory mediators, which are crucial for maintaining neural homeostasis. Key proteins: CD209.
    Read Context ▽

    Phagocytosis in the context of Parkinson's disease involves the microglial clearance of alpha-synuclein aggregates, myelin debris, and inflammatory mediators, which are crucial for maintaining neural homeostasis. The MER proto-oncogene, tyrosine kinase, plays a significant role in mediating the uptake of alpha-synuclein fibrils by microglia, potentially limiting aggregate spread without inducing inflammation. Inhibition of certain checkpoints, such as cluster of differentiation twenty-two, can enhance microglial clearance of these aggregates, suggesting a therapeutic target for restoring protective phagocytosis in the aging brain. Transcriptomic analyses have identified a unique microglial state in Parkinson's disease dementia, characterized by increased scavenger receptor expression and myelin phagocytosis, indicating that phagocytosis may also involve responses to myelin injury. Overall, while phagocytosis can be protective by clearing harmful debris, excessive or misdirected activity may contribute to neurodegeneration, highlighting its dual role in Parkinson's disease pathology.

    The TYROBP causal network in microglia is an innate immune signaling pathway that connects extracellular danger-sensing receptors to intracellular activation programs, playing a crucial role in transitioning microglia from a homeostatic to an inflammatory state. This pathway is involved in various neurodegenerative conditions, including Alzheimer's disease, where its activation leads to increased expression of immune-related genes and influences disease pathology. Although direct evidence linking this network to Parkinson's disease is limited, it is considered part of a conserved microglial immune module shared across aging and neurodegeneration, including Parkinson's. Integrin subunit beta 2, a component of this network, is implicated in Parkinson's disease risk, highlighting the pathway's relevance to microglial lysosomal and trafficking dysfunction, which are central to Parkinson's pathogenesis. Imaging transcriptomics further supports the association between microglial activation and neurodegeneration in Parkinson's, suggesting that immune and inflammatory processes are integral to the disease's progression.

    10. Integrin cell surface interactions (REAC:R-HSA-216083)

    Agent Summary: Integrin cell surface interactions play a crucial role in cell adhesion and signal transduction by linking extracellular matrix ligands to intracellular signaling networks, which is significant in the context of Parkinson's disease.
    Read Context ▽

    Integrin cell surface interactions play a crucial role in cell adhesion and signal transduction by linking extracellular matrix ligands to intracellular signaling networks, which is significant in the context of Parkinson's disease. These interactions involve integrin receptors, which are heterodimers composed of alpha and beta subunits, and their activation is influenced by factors such as talin binding and membrane lipid composition. In Parkinson's disease, integrins are implicated in neuroinflammation, blood-central nervous system barrier regulation, and the control of glial and immune cell behavior, making them relevant therapeutic targets. Integrins regulate leukocyte adhesion and trafficking, contributing to inflammatory processes in the disease, and are essential for maintaining blood-central nervous system barrier integrity, with disruptions potentially leading to barrier leakage and increased neuroinflammation. Additionally, integrin pathways influence immune cell behavior, with studies showing that targeting these pathways can reduce oxidative stress and promote an anti-inflammatory state in microglia, thereby improving dopaminergic pathology in Parkinson's models.
