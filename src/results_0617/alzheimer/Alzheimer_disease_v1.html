Introduction

Alzheimer disease (Disease code: I9_ALZHEIMER) is a progressive neurodegenerative disorder characterized by declining memory and cognition together with widespread synaptic, neuronal, and glial dysfunction. In the analysis of the United Kingdom Biobank Pharma Proteomics Project data, circulating levels of 10 plasma proteins were found to be significantly associated with Alzheimer disease, based on 52,344 controls and 676 cases, after application of Bonferroni correction at a stringent threshold of P < 0.000000001. This study population comprised 53,020 total participants, with cases representing approximately 1.27 percent of the cohort and controls representing approximately 98.73 percent, providing a large population framework for detecting robust protein-disease associations.

Understanding the molecular mechanisms reflected by these 10 disease-associated proteins is important for clarifying how Alzheimer disease emerges and progresses at the systems level. Although the protein set is numerically compact, it can still capture biologically coherent signals if the associated proteins converge on shared cellular programs. To investigate this possibility, we performed a comprehensive functional enrichment analysis on the 10 proteins, screening pathway annotations from Gene Ontology Molecular Function, Gene Ontology Biological Process, Gene Ontology Cellular Component, Reactome, and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes. Across these resources, we identified 27 significantly enriched biological pathways (Table 1), indicating that the observed protein associations are not randomly distributed but instead cluster within specific functional themes.

The enriched pathways point to a molecular landscape strongly centered on neuronal communication and structural integrity, while also implicating inflammatory regulation, receptor-mediated signaling, and cytoskeletal organization. In particular, the top signals include axon, neuronal system, synaptic vesicle pathway, syntaxin-1 binding, blood based neuroinflammation markers, intermediate filament, intermediate filament organization, signaling receptor activator activity, nuclear signaling by Erb-B2 receptor tyrosine kinase 4, and regulation of developmental growth, with reported enrichment probability values ranging from 0.00110 to 0.01807 in the leading pathways. Quantitatively, the highest ranked pathways were supported by 2 to 3 associated proteins each, suggesting repeated convergence of the 10-protein set on synaptic, axonal, inflammatory, and growth-related functions rather than dispersion across unrelated processes. This pattern is biologically consistent with current understanding of Alzheimer disease as a disorder involving coupled disturbances in synaptic transmission, axonal maintenance, neuroimmune activity, and cellular signaling.

Taken together, these enriched pathways provide a focused view of the molecular framework underlying Alzheimer disease in this dataset. In the following sections, we examine the most significant pathways and their protein interaction patterns in greater detail to define the biological processes most strongly linked to the disease-associated plasma proteomic signal.

axon (GO:0030424)

The axon is the elongated neuronal projection specialized for the initiation, propagation, and long-range transmission of electrical signals from the neuronal cell body to presynaptic terminals. Its function depends on a highly ordered subcellular architecture that includes the axon initial segment, where action potentials are generated, the shaft that supports polarized intracellular trafficking, and distal terminals that mediate neurotransmitter release. Normal axonal physiology requires intact cytoskeletal organization, efficient transport of vesicles, endolysosomal organelles, mitochondria, and proteins, as well as close structural and metabolic interactions with myelin and oligodendrocytes. Because axons can extend over long distances and rely on continuous trafficking and membrane maintenance, they are particularly vulnerable to disturbances in protein homeostasis, lipid handling, membrane integrity, and excitability. In neurodegenerative disease, axonal pathology commonly manifests as transport defects, focal swellings, dystrophic neurites, conduction failure, degeneration of long-range projections, and disruption of network connectivity. Thus, the axon is not merely a passive conduit for neuronal signaling, but a dynamic and metabolically demanding compartment whose structural and functional integrity is essential for cognition and circuit stability.

Against this biological background, the collected literature suggests that axonal dysfunction is a central and mechanistically diverse feature of Alzheimer disease, linking amyloid pathology, tau pathology, myelin injury, neuroinflammation, and neural network failure. A major theme is the formation of plaque-associated axonal spheroids and dystrophic neurites, which are now understood to be active drivers of circuit dysfunction rather than simple by-products of degeneration. In a landmark study, enlarging spheroids were shown to accumulate large endolysosomal vesicles enriched in phospholipase D family member three, and these swellings produced action potential conduction block by acting as electrical current sinks; reducing phospholipase D family member three decreased spheroid size and improved long-range axonal conduction and network function, demonstrating a direct causal role for axonal pathology in Alzheimer-related dysfunction independent of amyloid clearance [1]. Consistent with this, subcellular proteomics and induced pluripotent stem cell modeling identified abnormal protein turnover, cytoskeletal dynamics, and lipid transport within spheroids, together with activation of phosphoinositide three-kinase, protein kinase B, and mechanistic target of rapamycin signaling, and inhibition of mechanistic target of rapamycin ameliorated spheroid pathology, further supporting the therapeutic relevance of axonal compartments [2]. Several studies also indicate that impaired axonal transport is a key upstream mechanism. The Swedish amyloid precursor protein variant disrupts transport directionality, enhances retrograde bias, enlarges early endosomes, and impairs lysosome trafficking, thereby producing broad derangement of axonal transport pathways [3]. In parallel, amyloid precursor protein itself alters the position and length of the axon initial segment in mouse and human neurons, and similar shortening is observed in human sporadic Alzheimer disease, suggesting that excess amyloid precursor protein can directly remodel the site of action potential initiation and depress neuronal excitability independently of amyloid beta peptide [4]. Tau pathology further contributes to axonal injury: human cortical neurons with hyperphosphorylated tau display altered axon initial segment geometry [5], and tau seeding in primary neurons induces early axonopathy, cargo accumulation in dystrophic axons, and network hyperexcitability before overt cell loss [6]. Moreover, selective reduction of tau in axons ameliorates both phospho-tau and amyloid burden in an Alzheimer mouse model, indicating that axonal tau is not only a marker but also a mediator of disease progression [7]. The axon is also tightly linked to myelin pathology in Alzheimer disease. Proteomic and imaging analyses of human and mouse brain reveal that the myelin-axon interface is a site of amyloid beta aggregation, disrupted neuroglial signaling, reduced paranode density, and abnormal myelination around dystrophic axons [8]. Complementing this observation, myelin dysfunction promotes amyloid deposition by causing accumulation of amyloid-producing machinery within axonal swellings and by diverting microglial responses away from plaque containment, positioning myelin injury as an upstream driver of axonal and amyloid pathology [9]. Additional work shows that lithium deficiency accelerates amyloid beta deposition, phospho-tau accumulation, inflammatory activation, and loss of synapses, axons, and myelin, further emphasizing the vulnerability of axons to metabolic and signaling disturbances in Alzheimer disease [10]. At the level of membrane and vesicle biology, dystrophic neurites exhibit calcium elevation and calpain activation consistent with membrane injury, and annexin A6-mediated membrane repair reduces dystrophic neurite burden and phospho-tau accumulation, suggesting that axonal membrane damage is a mechanistic bridge between amyloid toxicity and tau phosphorylation [11]. Abnormal extracellular vesicle accumulation at presynaptic axon terminals and defects in primary cilia-dependent vesicle handling also promote axonal spheroid formation in vulnerable hippocampal neurons [12], while careful anatomical tracing indicates that most dystrophic neurites in a widely used mouse model arise from axon terminals, underscoring the distal axonal origin of much plaque-associated pathology [13]. Beyond local plaque-associated damage, long-range projection systems are affected early: locus coeruleus noradrenergic axons degenerate in the olfactory bulb before plaque appearance, driven in part by microglial phagocytosis, and this early axon loss contributes to prodromal olfactory dysfunction [14]. Biomarker studies align with these mechanistic findings by showing that markers of synaptic dysfunction rise earlier, whereas neurofilament light chain, an indicator of axonal degeneration, becomes abnormal later and tracks tau burden and cognitive decline, supporting a stage-dependent model in which axonal degeneration is a major determinant of progression and clinical deterioration [15] [16]. Overall, the evidence supports the conclusion that the axon is a major pathological substrate in Alzheimer disease, where defects in transport, excitability, membrane integrity, myelin interaction, vesicle processing, and tau localization converge to impair conduction, disconnect neural circuits, and accelerate neurodegeneration.[1] [8] [9] [10] [14] [15] [2] [11] [4] [16] [5] [6] [12] [13] [3] [7]

Nuclear signaling by ERBB4 (REAC:R-HSA-1251985)

Nuclear signaling by Erb-B2 receptor tyrosine kinase 4 is a proteolysis-dependent communication pathway in which a membrane receptor conveys extracellular information directly to the nucleus to regulate gene transcription. In this pathway, neuregulin binding activates Erb-B2 receptor tyrosine kinase 4 at the cell surface, after which the receptor undergoes sequential proteolytic processing that includes presenilin-dependent intramembrane cleavage by gamma-secretase. This cleavage releases the intracellular domain of Erb-B2 receptor tyrosine kinase 4, which then translocates to the nucleus and forms a transcriptional regulatory complex with TGF-beta-activated kinase 1 and MAP3K7-binding protein 2 and nuclear receptor corepressor 1. The resulting complex binds promoters of astrocytic differentiation genes and represses their expression, thereby delaying astrogenesis and maintaining neurogenic potential in developing neural precursors. This pathway is therefore an example of regulated intramembrane proteolysis serving not merely as a degradative event, but as a mechanism for generating a signaling-competent nuclear effector. Because the same presenilin-dependent gamma-secretase machinery also processes amyloid beta precursor protein and other substrates involved in developmental and transcriptional control, nuclear signaling by Erb-B2 receptor tyrosine kinase 4 may occupy an important intersection between neurodevelopment, transcriptional regulation, and proteolytic systems central to neurodegenerative disease biology.

With this mechanistic background in place, the potential relationship between this pathway and Alzheimer disease can be considered more directly. The relationship between nuclear signaling by Erb-B2 receptor tyrosine kinase 4 and Alzheimer disease arises primarily from the fact that this pathway depends on presenilin-dependent gamma-secretase activity, which is the same proteolytic system that generates amyloid beta from amyloid beta precursor protein and is therefore central to Alzheimer disease pathogenesis. The most direct evidence comes from the study showing that neuregulin-triggered cleavage of Erb-B2 receptor tyrosine kinase 4 releases an intracellular domain that enters the nucleus, associates with TGF-beta-activated kinase 1 and MAP3K7-binding protein 2 together with nuclear receptor corepressor 1, and represses astrocytic gene expression; importantly, the authors explicitly noted that this presenilin-dependent pathway may be relevant to understanding Alzheimer disease because it links presenilin function to transcriptional regulation and neural cell fate decisions rather than only to amyloid production [22]. This concept is reinforced by broader analyses of regulated intramembrane proteolysis, which identified amyloid beta precursor protein and Erb-B2 receptor tyrosine kinase 4 as parallel substrates of gamma-secretase and emphasized that cleavage products from such substrates can function in nuclear signaling, suggesting that Alzheimer disease biology may involve not only toxic peptide generation but also altered intracellular signaling and gene regulation when presenilin activity is perturbed [23] [24]. Additional evidence that Erb-B2 receptor tyrosine kinase 4 belongs to a wider presenilin-controlled substrate network comes from work showing that multiple membrane proteins undergo analogous presenilin-dependent cleavage to liberate intracellular domains for signaling, underscoring that therapeutic or pathogenic changes in gamma-secretase activity are likely to affect many pathways beyond amyloid beta precursor protein alone [25]. Taken together, these studies support a model in which Alzheimer disease-associated alterations in presenilin or gamma-secretase function could disrupt nuclear signaling by Erb-B2 receptor tyrosine kinase 4, thereby influencing neurodevelopmental programs, glial differentiation, and transcriptional homeostasis in the brain, while also complicating efforts to therapeutically inhibit gamma-secretase because such interventions may inadvertently impair beneficial Erb-B2 receptor tyrosine kinase 4 signaling.[22] [23] [24] [25]

Blood based neuroinflammation markers (WP:WP5548)

Blood based neuroinflammation markers comprise circulating molecules that reflect immune activation, glial reactivity, tissue injury, and neurovascular dysfunction associated with brain disease. In the context of Alzheimer disease, this pathway is better viewed not as a single linear biochemical cascade, but rather as a convergent biomarker framework that links peripheral blood measurements to central inflammatory processes accompanying amyloid deposition, tau pathology, synaptic dysfunction, axonal injury, and blood brain barrier alteration. The most informative markers highlighted in the provided literature include glial fibrillary acidic protein, chitinase three like one, soluble Axl, neurofilament light, phosphorylated tau species, inflammatory extracellular vesicle micro ribonucleic acids, and blood deoxyribonucleic acid methylation signatures related to inflammatory genes such as chitinase three like one. Glial fibrillary acidic protein is primarily interpreted as a marker of astrocytic activation and appears to increase with disease progression and cognitive decline. Chitinase three like one reflects a distinct astrocyte associated inflammatory response and may be especially informative in earlier stages, although its relationship to pathology appears to depend on disease stage. Soluble Axl belongs to the TAM receptor family and may index immune signaling linked to microglial activity and early degeneration of vulnerable brainstem nuclei. Neurofilament light reflects axonal injury rather than inflammation alone, but it is often integrated into neuroinflammatory biomarker panels because inflammatory injury and neurodegeneration are closely coupled in Alzheimer disease. Peripheral epigenetic signatures and extracellular vesicle micro ribonucleic acids further extend this pathway by suggesting that systemic immune regulation and neuron derived signaling may be captured in blood. Overall, blood based neuroinflammation markers are important because they offer minimally invasive access to dynamic disease biology, may support early detection and staging, may help distinguish mechanistic subtypes, and may serve as pharmacodynamic or prognostic tools in therapeutic development.

Building on this conceptual framework, the provided publications collectively suggest that blood based neuroinflammation markers are closely related to Alzheimer disease because neuroinflammation is now understood as an active component of disease pathogenesis rather than merely a downstream consequence of amyloid and tau accumulation. Review level evidence emphasizes that chronic dysregulated inflammation links amyloid beta aggregation, tau hyperphosphorylation, vascular dysfunction, and blood brain barrier impairment, thereby creating a bidirectional pathogenic loop that may be partially monitored in blood through glial markers, cytokine related candidates, inflammasome associated molecules, and extracellular vesicle signals [28] [29] [30]. Neuronal System (REAC:R-HSA-112316)

The neuronal system pathway encompasses the cellular and molecular processes that enable neurons to generate, transmit, integrate, and adapt electrical and chemical signals across brain circuits. This pathway includes excitatory and inhibitory synaptic transmission, neurotransmitter release and recycling, receptor signaling, dendritic spine maintenance, axonal transport, endosomal and lysosomal trafficking, organelle communication, activity-dependent plasticity, and the coordinated support provided by astrocytes, oligodendrocytes, microglia, and other glial populations to preserve neuronal homeostasis. In the healthy brain, these processes sustain learning, memory, sensory processing, and adaptive behavior through balanced circuit excitability and stable synaptic connectivity. In the context of Alzheimer disease, the neuronal system is not only a passive target of amyloid beta and tau pathology, but also an active participant in disease initiation and progression. Alterations in neuronal excitability, synaptic coupling, vesicle trafficking, endosomal function, glutamatergic signaling, cholinergic integrity, inhibitory interneuron support, mitochondrial and lysosomal homeostasis, and neuron to glia communication collectively reshape vulnerable neural circuits. Thus, the neuronal system pathway provides an integrative framework for understanding how molecular pathology may be translated into circuit dysfunction, selective neuronal vulnerability, memory impairment, and progressive neurodegeneration.

Building on this conceptual framework, the collected studies further suggest that the neuronal system pathway is centrally involved in Alzheimer disease because early and progressive defects in neuronal activity, synaptic communication, intracellular trafficking, and neuron to glia interactions appear to link amyloid beta accumulation and tau pathology to cognitive decline. A particularly important observation is that early cortical Alzheimer pathology is associated with a transient hyperactive neuronal state that precedes the loss of excitatory neurons, together with increased expression of genes related to amyloid beta production and processing in neurons and oligodendrocytes, suggesting that circuit hyperexcitability is an early disease feature rather than a late consequence [39] [40]. This theme is reinforced by analyses of intercellular communication showing conserved disease-related changes in glutamatergic transmission, metabotropic glutamate receptor signaling, and synaptic adhesion pathways in both human tissue and mouse models, supporting the view that excitotoxic and synaptic signaling abnormalities are core features of neuronal system disruption in Alzheimer disease [41]. At the same time, amyloid beta does not simply drive hyperactivity, but also appears to promote neuronal silencing through synaptic decoupling, with severe loss of presynaptic connectivity, dendritic spines, and synaptic activity in silent neurons, thereby providing a direct mechanistic link between amyloid beta pathology and circuit disintegration [42]. Several studies further indicate that neuronal intracellular trafficking pathways are major determinants of this dysfunction: neuronal endosomal dysregulation, including increased activity of early endosomal machinery, produces enlarged endosomes, abnormal synaptic endocytosis, impaired long-term potentiation and long-term depression, cholinergic neurodegeneration, and memory deficits, while genetic studies identify Alzheimer risk genes that converge on neuronal endosomal function and memory regulation [43] [44]. Neuronal vulnerability also appears to be selective rather than uniform, as transcriptomic studies identify tau-vulnerable neuronal populations enriched for genes involved in apoptosis, autophagosome maturation failure, actin filament disassembly, and vesicle-mediated transport defects, and comparative regional analyses suggest that differences in cholesterol homeostasis, antioxidant defenses, and related signaling pathways may help explain why some neuronal populations degenerate earlier than others in Alzheimer disease [45] [46]. Beyond neurons alone, the neuronal system pathway is also shaped by glial support and inflammatory crosstalk: astrocyte dysfunction impairs glutamate uptake, lactate release, and support of dendritic spine density, thereby weakening neuronal resilience, while inflammatory monocytes and activated microglia promote astrocyte activation and neuronal apoptosis, indicating that disturbed neuron glia communication may amplify neuronal system failure [47] [48] [49]. Additional evidence implicates inhibitory circuit instability, as modulation of leucine-rich repeat and immunoglobulin-like domain-containing nogo receptor-interacting protein 1 affects cannabinoid type 1 receptor and tyrosine kinase receptor B signaling and the survival of gamma aminobutyric acid interneurons, linking neuronal system imbalance to memory impairment [50]. Finally, studies of tau proteolysis and organelle crosstalk suggest that mitochondrial dysfunction, lysosomal impairment, and altered communication between intracellular compartments may further compromise synaptic and neuronal integrity, integrating bioenergetic failure with neurodegeneration [51] [52]. Taken together, these findings suggest that the neuronal system pathway represents a major biological axis in Alzheimer disease, connecting early hyperexcitability, synaptic failure, endosomal and vesicular dysfunction, selective neuronal vulnerability, glial dysregulation, and eventual neuronal death within a unified pathogenic framework.[39] [40] [44] [47] [43] [46] [45] [48] [42] [50] [41] [49] [51] [52]

Synaptic vesicle pathway (WP:WP2267)

The synaptic vesicle pathway comprises the molecular processes that generate, traffic, dock, fuse, recycle, and refill synaptic vesicles at presynaptic terminals. In physiological neurotransmission, synaptic vesicles are loaded with neurotransmitters, recruited to active zones, primed for exocytosis, and triggered to fuse with the presynaptic membrane in response to calcium influx through voltage-gated calcium channels. intermediate filament (GO:0005882)

Intermediate filaments are one of the three principal cytoskeletal systems of eukaryotic cells and form elongated fibers of approximately ten nanometers in diameter. In contrast to actin filaments and microtubules, intermediate filaments are assembled from chemically diverse proteins whose expression is highly cell-type specific, allowing them to provide both structural resilience and specialized biological functions. The family includes keratins, vimentin, desmin, neurofilaments, glial fibrillary acidic protein, and lamins. Their classical role is to confer mechanical stability, maintain cellular shape, distribute intracellular forces, and integrate the organization of the cytoplasm and nucleus. However, intermediate filaments are now understood to participate more broadly in organelle positioning, vesicular trafficking, signaling, stress responses, and cell-state transitions. In the nervous system, neurofilament proteins are major structural elements of axons and are essential for axonal caliber, conduction properties, and long-range neuronal integrity, whereas glial fibrillary acidic protein and vimentin are key intermediate filament proteins of astrocytes and are strongly remodeled during reactive astrogliosis. Because intermediate filaments are abundant, relatively stable, and released during cellular injury or remodeling, several of their components can be detected in cerebrospinal fluid or blood and therefore serve as measurable indicators of neuronal and glial pathology. In the context of Alzheimer disease, the intermediate filament pathway is especially relevant because it captures two central processes of disease biology: neuroaxonal degeneration, reflected particularly by neurofilament light chain, and astrocyte reactivity, reflected particularly by glial fibrillary acidic protein and, in some contexts, vimentin-related remodeling.

Intermediate filament organization is the cellular process that controls how intermediate filament proteins are assembled, cross-linked, positioned, and maintained within the cytoplasm to form stable but adaptable structural networks. In the nervous system, this pathway is especially important because intermediate filaments include neuron-enriched neurofilament proteins and glial intermediate filaments such as vimentin and glial fibrillary acidic protein. Proper organization of intermediate filaments is therefore essential for axonal transport, synaptic maintenance, resistance to mechanical and metabolic stress, and coordinated communication between neurons and glia. Disruption of this pathway can produce filament bundling, abnormal phosphorylation states, altered cross-linking, detergent-insoluble aggregates, and collapse or redistribution of the filament network, all of which are features broadly associated with neurodegeneration. In Alzheimer disease, the pathway is relevant at several levels: neurofibrillary lesions contain not only tau-associated paired helical filaments but also neurofilament-related antigens within straight filaments and transitional filament forms; cortical axonal architecture marked by neurofilament proteins becomes disorganized; circulating neurofilament light chain rises as a marker of ongoing axonal injury; and glial and neuronal cytoskeletal disturbances are linked to selective regional vulnerability, impaired intracellular organization, and degeneration.

Building on this biological framework, the collected literature suggests that intermediate filament organization may be related to Alzheimer disease through both direct structural pathology and broader consequences of neuronal and glial cytoskeletal failure. Early ultrastructural and immunocytochemical work showed that Alzheimer neurofibrillary tangles contain straight filaments in addition to paired helical filaments, and that these straight filaments share antigenic properties with tau and the two-hundred kilodalton neurofilament subunit, supporting the view that they arise from alternative modes of organizing a common abnormal filament system rather than from entirely distinct components [112]. Related studies further demonstrated abnormal accumulation of phosphorylated neurofilament epitopes in neurofibrillary pathology, reinforcing the idea that altered intermediate filament assembly state and post-translational regulation are mechanistically relevant to filament reorganization in degenerating neurons [113]. At the tissue level, neurofilament-based staining revealed that the normally ordered modular arrangement of cortical axon bundles is markedly disrupted in the prefrontal cortex in Alzheimer disease, even at early stages, indicating that disease progression involves large-scale breakdown of intermediate filament-supported axonal architecture [114]. Genetic and transcriptomic analysis also linked altered genes involved in intermediate filament organization to the temporal lobe in Alzheimer disease, with the authors proposing that disruption of glial structural integrity and glia-neuron communication contributes to selective vulnerability of affected regions [115]. Additional mechanistic support comes from studies showing that extracellular Alzheimer paired helical filaments can induce disturbed cytoskeletal organization involving dynein and vimentin, and from aggresome-related work showing that proteostasis failure can drive detergent-insoluble aggregates together with disruption of the intermediate filament network, thereby connecting filament disorganization with impaired protein clearance pathways that are central to Alzheimer pathobiology [116] [117]. Although blood neurofilament light chain does not directly measure filament organization, its consistent elevation in Alzheimer disease and its association with reduced functional connectivity and worse cognition strongly support the conclusion that ongoing injury to neurofilament-rich axons is a major downstream consequence of cytoskeletal disintegration in the disease [100] [118] [119]. Taken together, these studies support a model in which disturbed intermediate filament organization is not a peripheral feature of Alzheimer disease but an integral component of neuronal and glial degeneration, linking abnormal filament assembly, altered phosphorylation, axonal network disarray, impaired proteostasis, regional vulnerability, and cognitive decline.[112] [113] [114] [115] [116] [117] [100] [118] [119]

regulation of developmental growth (GO:0048638)

Regulation of developmental growth refers to the set of biological processes that adjust the timing, rate, magnitude, and spatial pattern of organismal and tissue growth during development. This pathway is not restricted to whole body size control, but also includes mechanisms that govern proliferation of progenitor cells, differentiation, maturation of neural and glial lineages, tissue patterning, vascular development, synapse formation, and establishment of supportive microenvironments. In the nervous system, regulation of developmental growth is mediated by interconnected signaling networks such as growth factor pathways, transforming growth factor beta family signaling, mitogen-activated protein kinase signaling, phosphatidylinositol 3 kinase and protein kinase B signaling, cell cycle regulators, extracellular matrix cues, and metabolic signals. These systems coordinate neurogenesis, axon and dendrite development, myelination, microglial maturation, blood-brain barrier formation, and assembly of functional neuronal circuits. Because the same developmental growth programs are often reused in tissue maintenance, repair, plasticity, and aging, their dysregulation can have long-term consequences for brain vulnerability. In neurodegenerative disease, especially Alzheimer disease, altered developmental growth regulation may contribute to disease risk by shaping early brain architecture, synaptic reserve, glial identity, neurovascular stability, and proteostatic capacity, while in later life reactivation or maladaptation of these pathways can influence amyloid beta production, tau-related pathology, inflammation, and neuronal survival.

Against this biological background, the collected publications suggest that regulation of developmental growth may be related to Alzheimer disease through both life-course susceptibility mechanisms and more direct pathogenic signaling in the adult brain. Several studies support the idea that abnormal developmental growth and maturation of neural systems can create a brain state that is more vulnerable to later Alzheimer-like degeneration. In a sporadic Alzheimer-like rat model, delayed early postnatal synapse assembly and altered expression of synapse-related genes, many of them linked to glial functions, were observed in the prefrontal cortex and hippocampus, suggesting that impaired developmental construction of neural circuits may precede later pathology [120]. This interpretation is further supported by evidence that developmental signaling pathways controlling microglial identity remain important in disease contexts: radial glia-dependent activation of transforming growth factor beta signaling was shown to be necessary for proper microglial development, and the authors explicitly proposed that such developmental programs may be reused in brain injury or disease, which is particularly relevant because microglial dysfunction is widely considered an important driver of Alzheimer pathology [121]. Additional publications point to specific growth-regulatory mediators with plausible Alzheimer relevance. Activin, a multifunctional regulator of proliferation, differentiation, reproduction, and early development, has been proposed as a downstream component of the adiponectin paradox and a potential contributor to Alzheimer disease through transforming growth factor beta family receptor signaling and pathological phosphorylation-related mechanisms [122]. Dual specificity tyrosine phosphorylation regulated kinase one alpha, a kinase with established roles in developmental brain regulation, has also been implicated in Alzheimer disease and has been discussed as a therapeutic target because its dysregulation may contribute to neurodegeneration and dementia-related processes [123]. Other studies connect growth-regulatory signaling more directly to Alzheimer mechanisms in the mature brain. Tenascin-R, an extracellular matrix protein with developmental functions, was shown in an amyloid precursor protein and presenilin one mouse model to enhance sodium channel activity, increase amyloid precursor protein and beta-site amyloid precursor protein cleaving enzyme one expression, promote amyloid beta generation, and worsen cognitive impairment, indicating that a matrix-linked growth and patterning molecule may be co-opted to drive amyloidogenic processing [124]. Exercise-related lactate receptor signaling and fibroblast growth factor twenty one signaling were reported to promote synaptic growth, cerebrovascular protection, and suppression of inflammatory synapse loss, suggesting that beneficial modulation of growth-associated plasticity pathways may counter early Alzheimer changes [125] [126] [73]. Taken together, these studies support a cautious model in which regulation of developmental growth may influence Alzheimer disease by determining early neural and glial maturation, shaping synaptic and vascular resilience, and supplying signaling modules that later modulate amyloid beta production, neuroinflammation, and neurodegeneration.[120] [121] [122] [123] [124] [125] [126] [73] [127]
