Introduction

Alzheimer disease (Disease code: I9_ALZHEIMER) is a progressive neurodegenerative disorder marked by declining memory and cognition, alongside widespread synaptic, neuronal, and glial dysfunction. This complex disease involves broad biological alterations, including disruptions in neuronal communication and inflammatory processes. In a comprehensive analysis of the United Kingdom Biobank Pharma Proteomics Project data, circulating levels of 10 plasma proteins were significantly associated with Alzheimer disease. This analysis included 52,344 controls and 676 cases, with a total cohort of 53,020 participants, and employed a stringent Bonferroni correction with a significance threshold of P < 0.000000001. The robust sample size and rigorous statistical approach provide a strong foundation for identifying meaningful protein-disease associations. Investigating these proteins is crucial for understanding the molecular mechanisms underlying Alzheimer disease, as they may reveal insights into disease heterogeneity, progression, and potential avenues for diagnosis or treatment. Despite the small number of proteins, their potential convergence on shared cellular pathways could illuminate key biological processes involved in the disease. To explore this, we conducted a functional enrichment analysis using pathway annotations from Gene Ontology Molecular Function, Gene Ontology Biological Process, Gene Ontology Cellular Component, Reactome, and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes. This analysis identified 27 significantly enriched biological pathways, suggesting that the associated proteins are not randomly distributed but instead cluster within specific functional themes. The enriched pathways highlight a molecular landscape centered on neuronal communication and structural integrity, implicating inflammatory regulation, receptor-mediated signaling, and cytoskeletal organization. Notably, pathways such as axon, neuronal system, synaptic vesicle pathway, syntaxin-1 binding, blood-based neuroinflammation markers, intermediate filament organization, signaling receptor activator activity, nuclear signaling by Erb-B2 receptor tyrosine kinase 4, and regulation of developmental growth were among the top signals, with enrichment P values ranging from 0.00110 to 0.01807. These pathways were supported by 2 to 3 associated proteins each, indicating a repeated convergence on synaptic, axonal, inflammatory, and growth-related functions, consistent with current understanding of Alzheimer disease. The literature-evidence layer integrates 76 distinct PubMed references, ensuring that the narrative does not rely solely on the primary proteomic association study. Proteins such as SYT1, APOE, NEFL, SNAP25, and GFAP are frequently cited, reinforcing their relevance to Alzheimer disease. Additionally, the STRING protein-protein interaction network analysis retained 9 high-confidence edges among 5 proteins, with a mean interaction score of 0.69. Proteins like GFAP, NEFL, SNAP25, APOE, and SYT1 emerged as highly connected nodes, suggesting coordinated interaction neighborhoods that complement the enrichment analysis. Overall, these findings provide a focused view of the molecular framework underlying Alzheimer disease, emphasizing the interconnected roles of synaptic transmission, axonal maintenance, neuroimmune activity, and cellular signaling. In the following sections, we delve deeper into the most significant pathways and their protein interaction patterns to further elucidate the biological processes strongly linked to the disease-associated plasma proteomic signal.     1. Nuclear signaling by ERBB4 (REAC:R-HSA-1251985)

    Agent Summary: The 'Nuclear signaling by ERBB4' pathway involves a proteolysis-dependent mechanism where the Erb-B2 receptor tyrosine kinase 4, upon activation by neuregulin binding, undergoes gamma-secretase-mediated cleavage. Pathway members (enrichment overlap): APOE, GFAP. [22] [23] [24]
    Read Context ▽

    Nuclear signaling by Erb-B2 receptor tyrosine kinase 4 is a proteolysis-dependent communication pathway in which a membrane receptor conveys extracellular information directly to the nucleus to regulate gene transcription. In this pathway, neuregulin binding activates Erb-B2 receptor tyrosine kinase 4 at the cell surface, after which the receptor undergoes sequential proteolytic processing that includes presenilin-dependent intramembrane cleavage by gamma-secretase. This cleavage releases the intracellular domain of Erb-B2 receptor tyrosine kinase 4, which then translocates to the nucleus and forms a transcriptional regulatory complex with TGF-beta-activated kinase 1 and MAP3K7-binding protein 2 and nuclear receptor corepressor 1. The resulting complex binds promoters of astrocytic differentiation genes and represses their expression, thereby delaying astrogenesis and maintaining neurogenic potential in developing neural precursors. This pathway is therefore an example of regulated intramembrane proteolysis serving not merely as a degradative event, but as a mechanism for generating a signaling-competent nuclear effector. Because the same presenilin-dependent gamma-secretase machinery also processes amyloid beta precursor protein and other substrates involved in developmental and transcriptional control, nuclear signaling by Erb-B2 receptor tyrosine kinase 4 may occupy an important intersection between neurodevelopment, transcriptional regulation, and proteolytic systems central to neurodegenerative disease biology.

    4. Synaptic vesicle pathway (WP:WP2267)

    Agent Summary: The synaptic vesicle pathway is crucial for neurotransmission, involving the generation, trafficking, docking, fusion, recycling, and refilling of synaptic vesicles at presynaptic terminals. Pathway members (enrichment overlap): SNAP25, SYT1. Context proteins (upstream regulators / disease context, not direct members): APOE. [53] [54] [55]
    Read Context ▽

    The synaptic vesicle pathway comprises the molecular processes that generate, traffic, dock, fuse, recycle, and refill synaptic vesicles at presynaptic terminals. In physiological neurotransmission, synaptic vesicles are loaded with neurotransmitters, recruited to active zones, primed for exocytosis, and triggered to fuse with the presynaptic membrane in response to calcium influx through voltage-gated calcium channels. After release, vesicle membranes and proteins are retrieved through endocytosis, reacidified by vacuolar adenosine triphosphatase complexes, and reassembled for subsequent rounds of release. This pathway depends on coordinated action of vesicle structural proteins, calcium sensors, membrane fusion machinery, clathrin-mediated endocytic components, adenosine triphosphatase-dependent proton pumps, and trafficking regulators such as N-ethylmaleimide-sensitive factor. Beyond sustaining neurotransmission, the pathway also influences presynaptic proteostasis, surface trafficking of amyloid precursor protein, and the balance between amyloidogenic and non-amyloidogenic processing. Because synaptic efficacy, plasticity, and circuit stability rely on precise vesicle cycling, even subtle disturbances in this pathway can impair neuronal communication, alter network activity, and increase synapse vulnerability.

    Against this biological background, the collected studies suggest that the synaptic vesicle pathway may be centrally involved in Alzheimer disease because it lies at the intersection of early synaptic failure, amyloid beta generation, aberrant neuronal excitability, inflammatory synapse elimination, and biomarker development. Human spatial transcriptomic analysis of the hippocampus showed Alzheimer disease-associated synaptic disorganization together with increased synapse pruning programs and altered microglia-astrocyte communication, especially in vulnerable regions such as cornu ammonis 1, supporting the view that presynaptic dysfunction occurs within a broader synapse-destabilizing tissue environment [53]. Proteomic profiling of patient-derived brain organoids and their extracellular vesicles further identified early alterations in synaptic and neurotransmitter pathways, indicating that vesicle-related synaptic abnormalities may arise early and may contribute to clinical heterogeneity [54]. Mechanistically, a particularly strong link comes from work showing that amyloid precursor protein processing can occur through the synaptic vesicle cycle and that levetiracetam lowers amyloid beta forty-two by synaptic vesicle protein two A-dependent modulation of vesicle cycling, shifts amyloid precursor protein toward surface localization and non-amyloidogenic processing, restores vesicle cycling abnormalities, and reduces synapse loss in Alzheimer disease models; taken together, these findings suggest that the synaptic vesicle pathway may act upstream of amyloid pathology rather than merely downstream of it [55]. Complementing this observation, amyloid beta oligomers were shown to drive pathological enhancement of presynaptic calcium channel activity, thereby increasing action potential-evoked synaptic vesicle exocytosis and producing presynaptic dysfunction, which identifies abnormal vesicle release dynamics as a direct toxic consequence of soluble amyloid species [56]. Convergent transcriptomic and genetic analyses identified synaptic vesicle cycling-related genes including adenosine triphosphatase H+ transporting V1 subunit D, adenosine triphosphatase H+ transporting V1 subunit G2, clathrin light chain B, and N-ethylmaleimide-sensitive factor as downregulated and potentially protective in Alzheimer disease, linking impaired vesicle recycling, acidification, and membrane trafficking to disease risk and inflammatory states [57]. Additional biomarker-oriented studies similarly connected reduced ubiquitin conjugating enzyme E2 N to synaptic vesicle cycling and Alzheimer disease, reinforcing the possible clinical relevance of vesicle pathway disruption [58]. Therapeutic evidence is also consistent with this model: cerebrospinal fluid proteomics from a clinical trial of zervimesine identified treatment-responsive proteins related to synapses and vesicle trafficking that correlated with improved functional connectivity, suggesting that restoration of vesicle-associated synaptic biology is measurable in humans and may track therapeutic engagement [59]. Finally, several studies indicate that vesicle-related pathology may extend beyond neuronal terminals to extracellular vesicles and immune-mediated synapse loss: gut-derived bacterial vesicles carrying lipopolysaccharide promoted microglia-mediated complement-dependent synaptic pruning, while neuron-derived extracellular vesicles carrying apolipoprotein E impaired synapse formation and reduced synaptic protein expression, indicating that vesicular communication outside the classical presynaptic terminal can amplify synaptic degeneration in Alzheimer disease [60] [61]. Taken together, the evidence supports a cautious model in which disruption of the synaptic vesicle pathway may represent not merely a secondary epiphenomenon, but a core pathogenic process contributing to amyloid production, abnormal neurotransmitter release, synapse destabilization, neuroimmune pruning, and measurable clinical dysfunction in Alzheimer disease.     Read Context ▽

    Intermediate filament organization is the cellular process that controls how intermediate filament proteins are assembled, cross-linked, positioned, and maintained within the cytoplasm to form stable but adaptable structural networks. In the nervous system, this pathway is especially important because intermediate filaments include neuron-enriched neurofilament proteins and glial intermediate filaments such as vimentin and glial fibrillary acidic protein. These polymers do not simply provide passive mechanical support; rather, they determine axonal caliber, preserve neuronal and glial architecture, organize cytoplasmic domains, influence organelle positioning, and help integrate the intermediate filament system with microtubules, actin filaments, motor proteins, and protein quality control pathways. Proper organization of intermediate filaments is therefore essential for axonal transport, synaptic maintenance, resistance to mechanical and metabolic stress, and coordinated communication between neurons and glia. Disruption of this pathway can produce filament bundling, abnormal phosphorylation states, altered cross-linking, detergent-insoluble aggregates, and collapse or redistribution of the filament network, all of which are features broadly associated with neurodegeneration. In Alzheimer disease, the pathway is relevant at several levels: neurofibrillary lesions contain not only tau-associated paired helical filaments but also neurofilament-related antigens within straight filaments and transitional filament forms; cortical axonal architecture marked by neurofilament proteins becomes disorganized; circulating neurofilament light chain rises as a marker of ongoing axonal injury; and glial and neuronal cytoskeletal disturbances are linked to selective regional vulnerability, impaired intracellular organization, and degeneration.

    Read Context ▽

    Regulation of developmental growth refers to the set of biological processes that adjust the timing, rate, magnitude, and spatial pattern of organismal and tissue growth during development. This pathway is not restricted to whole body size control, but also includes mechanisms that govern proliferation of progenitor cells, differentiation, maturation of neural and glial lineages, tissue patterning, vascular development, synapse formation, and establishment of supportive microenvironments. In the nervous system, regulation of developmental growth is mediated by interconnected signaling networks such as growth factor pathways, transforming growth factor beta family signaling, mitogen-activated protein kinase signaling, phosphatidylinositol 3 kinase and protein kinase B signaling, cell cycle regulators, extracellular matrix cues, and metabolic signals. These systems coordinate neurogenesis, axon and dendrite development, myelination, microglial maturation, blood-brain barrier formation, and assembly of functional neuronal circuits. Because the same developmental growth programs are often reused in tissue maintenance, repair, plasticity, and aging, their dysregulation can have long-term consequences for brain vulnerability. In neurodegenerative disease, especially Alzheimer disease, altered developmental growth regulation may contribute to disease risk by shaping early brain architecture, synaptic reserve, glial identity, neurovascular stability, and proteostatic capacity, while in later life reactivation or maladaptation of these pathways can influence amyloid beta production, tau-related pathology, inflammation, and neuronal survival.

    Against this biological background, the collected publications suggest that regulation of developmental growth may be related to Alzheimer disease through both life-course susceptibility mechanisms and more direct pathogenic signaling in the adult brain. Several studies support the idea that abnormal developmental growth and maturation of neural systems can create a brain state that is more vulnerable to later Alzheimer-like degeneration. In a sporadic Alzheimer-like rat model, delayed early postnatal synapse assembly and altered expression of synapse-related genes, many of them linked to glial functions, were observed in the prefrontal cortex and hippocampus, suggesting that impaired developmental construction of neural circuits may precede later pathology [120]. This interpretation is further supported by evidence that developmental signaling pathways controlling microglial identity remain important in disease contexts: radial glia-dependent activation of transforming growth factor beta signaling was shown to be necessary for proper microglial development, and the authors explicitly proposed that such developmental programs may be reused in brain injury or disease, which is particularly relevant because microglial dysfunction is widely considered an important driver of Alzheimer pathology [121]. Additional publications point to specific growth-regulatory mediators with plausible Alzheimer relevance. Activin, a multifunctional regulator of proliferation, differentiation, reproduction, and early development, has been proposed as a downstream component of the adiponectin paradox and a potential contributor to Alzheimer disease through transforming growth factor beta family receptor signaling and pathological phosphorylation-related mechanisms [122]. Dual specificity tyrosine phosphorylation regulated kinase one alpha, a kinase with established roles in developmental brain regulation, has also been implicated in Alzheimer disease and has been discussed as a therapeutic target because its dysregulation may contribute to neurodegeneration and dementia-related processes [123]. Other studies connect growth-regulatory signaling more directly to Alzheimer mechanisms in the mature brain. Tenascin-R, an extracellular matrix protein with developmental functions, was shown in an amyloid precursor protein and presenilin one mouse model to enhance sodium channel activity, increase amyloid precursor protein and beta-site amyloid precursor protein cleaving enzyme one expression, promote amyloid beta generation, and worsen cognitive impairment, indicating that a matrix-linked growth and patterning molecule may be co-opted to drive amyloidogenic processing [124]. Exercise-related lactate receptor signaling and fibroblast growth factor twenty one signaling were reported to promote synaptic growth, cerebrovascular protection, and suppression of inflammatory synapse loss, suggesting that beneficial modulation of growth-associated plasticity pathways may counter early Alzheimer changes [125] [126] [73]. Finally, evidence from Down syndrome is informative because this condition confers a high risk of Alzheimer-type neurodegeneration; insulin-like growth factor one deficiency was linked to both stunted growth and neurodegeneration, supporting the broader principle that disturbed growth regulation can be mechanistically coupled to later neurodegenerative vulnerability [127]. Taken together, these studies support a cautious model in which regulation of developmental growth may influence Alzheimer disease by determining early neural and glial maturation, shaping synaptic and vascular resilience, and supplying signaling modules that later modulate amyloid beta production, neuroinflammation, and neurodegeneration.
