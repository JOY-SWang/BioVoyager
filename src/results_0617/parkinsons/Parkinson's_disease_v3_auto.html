Introduction

Parkinson's disease (PD), coded as PARKINSONS, is a progressive neurodegenerative disorder primarily characterized by the loss of dopaminergic neurons, abnormal protein aggregation, and a spectrum of motor and non-motor dysfunctions. The disease is marked by broad biological alterations, including neuroimmune activation and tissue-level remodeling. In a comprehensive analysis of the UK Biobank Pharma Proteomics Project data, we identified significant associations between circulating levels of 19 plasma proteins and Parkinson's disease. This analysis involved a large cohort of 52,100 controls and 874 cases, with significance determined using a stringent Bonferroni-corrected threshold of P < 0.000000001. Understanding the roles of these proteins is crucial for elucidating the underlying mechanisms of Parkinson's disease, which may reveal insights into disease heterogeneity, progression, and potential avenues for diagnosis or treatment. To explore these associations further, we conducted a functional enrichment analysis using several pathway databases, including Gene Ontology Molecular Function, Gene Ontology Biological Process, Gene Ontology Cellular Component, Reactome, and the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG). This analysis identified 59 significantly enriched biological pathways, highlighting key processes such as extracellular matrix organization, integrin-mediated adhesion, cell-surface signaling, phagocytosis, microglial immune activity, and broader stress-response and neuroendocrine processes. Representative pathways include extracellular matrix organization (Reactome, P = 0.003, 5 proteins), integrin cell surface interactions (Reactome, P = 0.000627, 4 proteins), and phagocytosis (GO Biological Process, P = 0.01578, 3 proteins). These findings suggest that Parkinson's disease involves complex interactions beyond neuron-intrinsic degeneration, encompassing altered neuron-microenvironment interactions, chronic inflammatory clearance mechanisms, adhesion-dependent signaling, and systemic neuroendocrine components. The literature-supported evidence layer integrates 79 distinct PubMed references, ensuring that the narrative does not solely depend on the primary proteomic association study. Notably, proteins such as ITGAV, ITGA11, TNXB, ITGAM, and ITGB2 are frequently mentioned in the literature, underscoring their potential significance in Parkinson's disease pathways. Furthermore, the STRING protein-protein interaction (PPI) network analysis retained 10 high-confidence network edges among 6 proteins, with a mean interaction score of 0.82. Proteins such as ITGA11, ITGAM, ITGAV, and ITGB2 emerged as highly connected nodes, suggesting coordinated interaction neighborhoods that complement the enrichment analysis findings. In summary, this multi-layered analysis provides a comprehensive view of the molecular framework associated with Parkinson's disease, highlighting potential targets for further investigation.     2. Integrin cell surface interactions (REAC:R-HSA-216083)

    Agent Summary: Integrin cell surface interactions are crucial for cell adhesion and signal transduction, linking the extracellular matrix to intracellular signaling networks and the cytoskeleton. Core family members: ITGA11, ITGAM, ITGAV, ITGB2. [98] [99] [100]
    Read Context ▽

    Integrin cell surface interactions comprise a fundamental cell adhesion and signal transduction pathway through which integrin receptors on the plasma membrane bind extracellular matrix ligands and couple the extracellular environment to intracellular cytoskeletal and signaling networks. Integrins are heterodimeric receptors formed by alpha and beta subunits, and their activation is tightly regulated by conformational change, membrane lipid composition, and recruitment of cytoplasmic adaptors such as talin. A key mechanistic study showed that talin binding to acidic membrane phospholipids is required for integrin activation and focal adhesion formation, indicating that integrin signaling depends not only on receptor expression but also on membrane-associated activation machinery and linkage to the actin cytoskeleton [98]. Once activated, integrins organize focal adhesions and transmit bidirectional signals that regulate cell survival, migration, inflammatory activation, barrier properties, cytoskeletal remodeling, vesicular trafficking, and responses to mechanical stress. This pathway also intersects with phosphatidylinositol 3 kinase and protein kinase B signaling, platelet-derived growth factor receptor signaling, Wnt-beta-catenin signaling, extracellular matrix remodeling, and immune cell adhesion programs [99] [100] [101] [102]. In the central nervous system, integrin-mediated interactions are especially important at the neurovascular interface, where pericyte-derived vitronectin acting through integrin alpha five in endothelial cells helps maintain blood-central nervous system barrier integrity by suppressing transcytosis [103]. More broadly, extracellular matrix molecules and their receptors, including integrins, have been recognized as relevant therapeutic targets across central nervous system disorders including Parkinson disease, because they influence neuroinflammation, tissue remodeling, and cell-cell communication within the diseased brain [104].

    Against this mechanistic background, the available literature supports a biologically plausible connection between integrin cell surface interactions and Parkinson disease through three converging processes: neuroinflammation, blood-central nervous system barrier regulation, and extracellular matrix-dependent control of glial and immune behavior. Although the cited studies are not centered exclusively on Parkinson disease, they define mechanisms that appear highly relevant to disease pathogenesis. First, integrins are major regulators of leukocyte adhesion and trafficking in inflammatory conditions, and a central nervous system disease review explicitly identifies integrins and other extracellular matrix receptors as contributors to Parkinson disease-related pathology and as candidate therapeutic targets [104]. This interpretation is consistent with evidence that inflammatory cell recruitment into diseased nervous tissue depends on adhesion systems such as intercellular adhesion molecule one and leukocyte integrins, a process noted in a report discussing neuroinflammatory responses in Parkinson disease [105]. Second, neurovascular dysfunction is increasingly recognized in Parkinson disease, and integrin signaling has direct relevance because vitronectin-integrin alpha five signaling between pericytes and endothelial cells is required for blood-central nervous system barrier integrity [103]. Disruption of such adhesive signaling could therefore facilitate barrier leakage, inflammatory mediator entry, and amplification of microglial activation. Third, integrin pathways shape innate immune cell behavior in Parkinson disease models: a therapeutic nanoparticle study demonstrated that microglial targeting in Parkinson disease can be achieved through vascular cell adhesion molecule one and alpha four beta one integrin interactions, and modulation of these cells reduced oxidative stress, shifted microglia toward an anti-inflammatory state, and improved dopaminergic pathological features in mice [106]. Mechanistically, these disease-relevant effects are credible because integrin activation depends on talin-mediated membrane engagement and focal adhesion assembly [98], while broader integrin signaling is known to control survival, migration, inflammatory signaling, and crosstalk with phosphatidylinositol 3 kinase and protein kinase B and other stress-response pathways [99] [102]. Taken together, the pathway may be related to Parkinson disease not as a single linear cause, but rather as a regulatory interface linking extracellular matrix alterations, vascular barrier stability, immune cell adhesion, and microglial inflammatory state, all of which could influence dopaminergic neuron vulnerability and disease progression. [98] [99] [102] [103] [104] [105] [106]

    3. TYROBP causal network in microglia (WP:WP3945)

    Agent Summary: The TYROBP causal network in microglia is an innate immune signaling pathway that connects extracellular receptors to intracellular activation programs, influencing microglial transition from a homeostatic to an inflammatory state. Pathway members (enrichment overlap): IL13RA1, ITGAM, ITGB2. [94] [95] [96]
    Read Context ▽

    The TYRO protein tyrosine kinase binding protein causal network in microglia is an innate immune signaling pathway centered on TYRO protein tyrosine kinase binding protein, a transmembrane adaptor that couples extracellular danger-sensing receptors on microglia to intracellular activation programs. This network is closely connected to triggering receptor expressed on myeloid cells 2, integrin signaling, complement activation, phagocytosis, lysosomal trafficking, and the transition of microglia from a homeostatic state to a disease-associated inflammatory state. Across multiple neurodegenerative and injury contexts, activation of this pathway is accompanied by increased expression of genes such as TYRO protein tyrosine kinase binding protein, triggering receptor expressed on myeloid cells 2, complement C1q components, integrin subunit beta 2, Fc fragment of immunoglobulin E receptor Ig, and other microglia-enriched immune genes. Experimental studies in Alzheimer disease models indicate that loss of TYRO protein tyrosine kinase binding protein suppresses disease-associated microglial programs, represses complement-related transcriptional changes, and improves electrophysiological and behavioral phenotypes even without reducing amyloid burden, supporting a causal role for this network in neuroimmune pathology rather than a merely reactive role. Additional work across aging, traumatic brain injury, epilepsy, and developmental microglia-like states further suggests that this pathway may function as a broad regulator of microglial activation, inflammatory communication, and phagocytic remodeling in the central nervous system.

    Against this biological background, the available literature supports an indirect but biologically credible link between the TYRO protein tyrosine kinase binding protein causal network in microglia and Parkinson disease. Although the strongest direct mechanistic evidence for this pathway comes from Alzheimer disease and other neuroinflammatory conditions, several cited studies indicate that TYRO protein tyrosine kinase binding protein sits within a conserved microglial immune module shared across aging and neurodegeneration, including Parkinson disease. In a cross-disease network analysis, TYRO protein tyrosine kinase binding protein was identified as a human hub gene within a microglia-enriched module associated with aging, Alzheimer disease, Parkinson disease, and Huntington disease, suggesting that this pathway may mark a common neuroimmune response state relevant to Parkinson disease biology as well as to other degenerative disorders [94]. This observation is notable because aging is the major risk factor for Parkinson disease, and the same study argues that microglial gene regulatory programs are preserved across species and disease contexts. Further support comes from Parkinson disease proteogenomic work showing that leucine rich repeat kinase 2 variants regulate a set of proteins enriched in microglia and lysosomal trafficking pathways, including cluster of differentiation 68 and integrin subunit beta 2, with integrin subunit beta 2 identified as a causal protein candidate for Parkinson disease risk [95]. Because integrin subunit beta 2 is a canonical component of the TYRO protein tyrosine kinase binding protein-associated microglial activation network, this finding places part of the pathway downstream of a major Parkinson disease genetic locus and links it to microglial lysosomal and trafficking dysfunction, processes already central to Parkinson disease pathogenesis. A review of imaging transcriptomics in neurodegenerative diseases further supports this interpretation by showing that regions vulnerable in Parkinson disease are enriched for immune, neuroinflammatory, and microglia-related gene expression programs, indicating that spatially patterned neurodegeneration is associated with innate immune activation rather than being purely neuron-autonomous [96]. At the same time, a commentary on substantia nigra single-nucleus data emphasizes that inherited Parkinson disease risk is more strongly associated with dopaminergic neurons and oligodendroglial lineage cells than with microglia, arguing against microglia as the primary cell type of genetic causation in Parkinson disease [97]. Taken together, the most balanced interpretation is that the TYRO protein tyrosine kinase binding protein causal network in microglia is unlikely to represent the principal initiating pathway in Parkinson disease, but it may still act as an important modifier of disease progression by amplifying inflammatory signaling, complement activity, phagocytic remodeling, and lysosomal stress in response to neuronal injury and genetic perturbations such as leucine rich repeat kinase 2 dysregulation. Within this framework, the pathway is best viewed as a secondary but potentially therapeutically relevant neuroimmune program that may influence the tempo and severity of dopaminergic neurodegeneration in Parkinson disease.     4. phagocytosis (GO:0006909)

    Agent Summary: Phagocytosis is a critical process in the nervous system, primarily executed by microglia, which involves engulfing and degrading extracellular material to maintain tissue homeostasis. Pathway members (enrichment overlap): CD209. [84] [85] [86]
    Read Context ▽

    Phagocytosis is a specialized vesicle-mediated process through which professional phagocytes engulf extracellular particulate material, enclose it within membrane-bound phagosomes, and subsequently fuse these compartments with lysosomes to degrade and recycle the ingested cargo. In the nervous system, this pathway is executed primarily by microglia, although astrocytes can also acquire phagocytic properties under pathological conditions. Phagocytosis is not limited to host defense against microbes; it is also essential for tissue homeostasis through the clearance of apoptotic cells, protein aggregates, damaged myelin, synaptic material, and other cellular debris. The process depends on sequential recognition of targets through surface receptors, cytoskeletal remodeling required for engulfment, phagosome formation, and phagolysosomal maturation for cargo digestion. Multiple receptor systems regulate these steps, including scavenger receptors, purinergic receptors, and the TYRO3, AXL, and MER proto-oncogene, tyrosine kinase family, which often mediate non-inflammatory clearance of phosphatidylserine-exposing material. In the brain, phagocytosis has a dual biological role: when appropriately regulated, it limits inflammation and promotes repair by removing toxic aggregates and debris; when impaired, harmful material accumulates and sustains inflammatory stress; when excessive or misdirected, viable neurons, synapses, or myelin may be inappropriately removed, thereby contributing to neurodegeneration. Thus, phagocytosis is best understood as a tightly regulated homeostatic and immunological pathway whose functional consequences depend on cell type, receptor context, metabolic state, and disease stage.

    Against this biological background, the collected literature suggests that phagocytosis is closely linked to Parkinson disease through microglial handling of alpha-synuclein aggregates, myelin debris, inflammatory tone, and Parkin-related regulation of engulfment machinery. The most direct evidence comes from the demonstration that MER proto-oncogene, tyrosine kinase mediates uptake of alpha-synuclein fibrils by human microglia; pharmacological inhibition or gene silencing reduced fibril internalization, whereas this pathway appeared non-inflammatory and may therefore help limit aggregate spread in synucleinopathies, including Parkinson disease [84]. Complementing this observation, inhibition of cluster of differentiation twenty-two restored microglial clearance of alpha-synuclein fibrils in vivo, indicating that anti-phagocytic checkpoints can restrain aggregate removal and that relieving such inhibition may re-establish protective homeostatic phagocytosis in the ageing brain, a major context for Parkinson disease vulnerability [85]. Human single-nucleus transcriptomic analysis across dementia subtypes further identified a distinct microglial state in Parkinson disease dementia characterized by increased macrophage scavenger receptor one expression and enhanced myelin phagocytosis, together with evidence of microglia-oligodendrocyte interaction, suggesting that phagocytosis in Parkinson-related dementia may extend beyond protein aggregate clearance to include responses to myelin injury and white matter remodeling [86]. More broadly, review and experimental studies support the view that microglial phagocytosis can be either protective or harmful in neurodegeneration: efficient clearance of debris and aggregates may promote resolution and repair, whereas excessive engulfment of stressed but viable neurons or synapses may amplify tissue loss [87] [88]. This duality appears particularly relevant to Parkinson disease, in which chronic microglial activation, neuronal stress signals, and extracellular alpha-synuclein may shift phagocytosis from beneficial clearance toward maladaptive pruning. A particularly important mechanistic link to Parkinson biology is provided by the report that Parkin, through its Caenorhabditis elegans homolog Parkinson disease related one, negatively regulates engulfment by promoting degradation of the Rac family small guanosine triphosphatase signaling component required for cytoskeletal rearrangement during corpse uptake; this finding connects Parkin directly to the cellular machinery governing phagocytosis and suggests that altered Parkin function could influence phagocyte behavior as well as neuronal vulnerability [89]. Finally, recent work showing that microglial mitochondrial state strongly correlates with expression of phagocytosis-relevant genes is also pertinent, because mitochondrial dysfunction is a central feature of Parkinson disease and may therefore secondarily reshape microglial phagocytic competence [90]. Taken together, the available evidence supports a model in which phagocytosis may represent a central interface between proteinopathy, glial immunity, myelin turnover, and Parkin-associated cytoskeletal regulation in Parkinson disease; depending on receptor usage and disease context, this pathway may either restrain pathology by clearing alpha-synuclein and debris or contribute to progression through maladaptive engulfment and glial-driven tissue remodeling. [84] [85] [86] [87] [88] [89] [90]

    5. Hippo Merlin signaling dysregulation (WP:WP4541)

    Agent Summary: The Hippo Merlin signaling pathway, centered around the protein Merlin, plays a crucial role in regulating cell growth, stress responses, and transcriptional activity through its influence on the yes-associated protein 1 (YAP1) and tafazzin. Pathway members (enrichment overlap): ITGA11, ITGAM, ITGAV, ITGB2. [78] [79] [80]
    Read Context ▽

    Hippo Merlin signaling dysregulation refers to disruption of a growth-control and stress-response network centered on Merlin, the protein encoded by neurofibromin 2, together with the core Hippo kinase cascade and the transcriptional coactivators yes-associated protein 1 and tafazzin. Under physiological conditions, Merlin functions upstream of the Hippo pathway to promote kinase activity that restrains yes-associated protein 1 and tafazzin, thereby limiting their nuclear entry and reducing transcription of genes involved in proliferation, survival, mechanical sensing, and cellular state transitions. When Merlin is lost or functionally impaired, Hippo pathway output declines and yes-associated protein 1 and tafazzin become aberrantly active, driving transcriptional programs through TEA domain family transcription factors. The provided literature indicates that this regulatory module is highly context dependent. In nervous system tumors caused by neurofibromin 2 loss, Hippo pathway dysregulation promotes Schwann cell and meningeal tumor growth, and pharmacologic inhibition of TEA domain activity suppresses this phenotype, suggesting that Merlin normally constrains pathogenic yes-associated protein 1 and tafazzin signaling in neural lineage cells [78] [79] [80]. Additional work further indicates that Merlin also exerts Hippo-independent effects, including regulation of lin-28 homolog B and let-7 microRNA maturation in a cell-density-dependent tumor-suppressive pathway, underscoring that Merlin integrates contact inhibition, cytoskeletal organization, and transcriptional control beyond the canonical kinase cascade [81]. More broadly, the pathway responds to environmental and tissue-architecture cues, influences differentiation and regenerative plasticity in Schwann cells, and can regulate inflammatory or secreted mediators such as cysteine rich angiogenic inducer 61 and connective tissue growth factor that reshape tissue microenvironments [82] [80]. Taken together, Hippo Merlin signaling dysregulation is most appropriately understood as a disturbance of a mechanosensitive and context-dependent signaling hub that links cell contact, cytoskeletal tension, transcriptional regulation, regeneration, and inflammation.

    Against this biological background, the relationship between Hippo Merlin signaling dysregulation and Parkinson disease appears indirect but biologically plausible, with support arising from convergent evidence in neurobiology and neuroinflammation rather than from direct mechanistic studies of Parkinson disease within the provided literature set. A recent review of cytokine-associated neuroinflammation in Parkinson disease explicitly identifies the Hippo pathway as one of the signaling systems with anti-inflammatory therapeutic relevance in Parkinson disease models, thereby placing this pathway within the broader inflammatory network that contributes to alpha-synuclein aggregation, glial activation, blood-brain barrier dysfunction, and dopaminergic neuron loss [83]. The remaining publications help clarify why this pathway could plausibly matter in Parkinson disease. Merlin and Hippo signaling regulate cell-state transitions, repair competence, and stress responses in Schwann cells, where Merlin loss causes abnormal yes-associated protein 1 activation and severe defects in regenerative function, while restoration of pathway restraint improves repair-related outcomes [80]. Related work further shows that Hippo signaling in peripheral glial cells is sensitive to environmental stress and tightly linked to changes in differentiation, proliferation, migration, and junctional integrity, all of which are conceptually relevant to glial dysfunction associated with neurodegenerative disease [82]. In nervous system tumors driven by neurofibromin 2 loss, aberrant activation of yes-associated protein 1, tafazzin, and TEA domain-dependent transcription is sufficient to produce pathological growth programs, demonstrating the potency of this pathway in neural-derived cells and supporting the view that dysregulated transcriptional outputs downstream of Merlin can profoundly alter cell behavior [78] [79]. Moreover, evidence from ependymoma links nuclear yes-associated protein and tafazzin activity with macrophage infiltration and expression of inflammatory target genes such as cysteine rich angiogenic inducer 61, suggesting that Hippo pathway dysregulation can shape immune microenvironments in the nervous system (no PubMed identifier available). Taken together, these studies support a cautious model in which Hippo Merlin signaling may be relevant to Parkinson disease by modulating neuroinflammatory tone, glial plasticity, stress adaptation, and regenerative responses rather than acting as a primary monogenic driver. Accordingly, in Parkinson disease, this pathway is best viewed as a candidate modifier pathway that may influence disease progression and therapeutic responsiveness through effects on inflammation and neural support-cell function. [83] [80] [82] [78] [79] [81]

    6. cell-matrix adhesion (GO:0007160)

    Agent Summary: Cell-matrix adhesion is a complex biological process that connects cells to the extracellular matrix via integrin-centered assemblies, influencing various cellular functions such as shape, migration, and survival. Pathway members (enrichment overlap): ITGA11, ITGAM, ITGAV, ITGB2. [69] [70] [71]
    Read Context ▽

    Cell-matrix adhesion is the biological process through which cells bind to components of the extracellular matrix by means of surface adhesion receptors and associated intracellular complexes. This process is mediated principally by integrin-centered adhesion assemblies that physically connect extracellular matrix ligands to the actin cytoskeleton and thereby couple tissue architecture to intracellular signaling. Rather than functioning only as a structural attachment system, this pathway is increasingly understood as a dynamic signaling platform that regulates cell shape, polarity, migration, survival, proliferation, membrane trafficking, organelle organization, and transcriptional responses to mechanical cues. Systems-level analyses describe cell-matrix adhesion sites as highly complex and rapidly remodeled molecular networks, often referred to as the integrin adhesome, whose composition determines how cells sense extracellular matrix composition and stiffness and convert these inputs into biochemical and mechanical outputs [69]. Experimental studies further show that cell-matrix adhesion controls tissue morphogenesis through beta one integrin-dependent interactions with basement membrane, demonstrating that the balance between cell-matrix and cell-cell adhesion can determine cellular behavior and tissue organization [70]. The cited literature indicates that focal adhesion kinase acts as a key integrator of signals from cell adhesion and growth factors, and that mechanical force can directly induce conformational changes that activate focal adhesion kinase signaling, thereby linking extracellular mechanical inputs to intracellular responses [23]. Additional studies suggest that focal adhesion assembly and turnover are tightly coordinated with transcriptional regulators of cell mechanics, including yes associated protein, which can control expression of integrins and focal adhesion docking proteins and thereby influence adhesion strength, force generation, cell shape, and migration [24] [25]. The pathway also appears to be functionally coupled to calcium dependent mechanosensing, as force dependent recruitment of piezo type mechanosensitive ion channel component 1 to adhesions promotes adhesion maturation and calcium entry [26]. Taken together, focal adhesion may be viewed as a core pathway through which cells integrate extracellular matrix contact, cytoskeletal tension, and signaling cascades to regulate motility, tissue repair, and responses to stress.
