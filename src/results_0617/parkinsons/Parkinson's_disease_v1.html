Introduction

Parkinson disease is a progressive neurodegenerative disorder characterized by dopaminergic neuronal loss, abnormal protein aggregation, and widespread motor and non-motor dysfunction (Disease code: PARKINSONS). In the analysis of the UK Biobank Pharma Proteomics Project data, circulating levels of 19 plasma proteins were significantly associated with Parkinson disease, based on 52,100 controls and 874 cases, after application of a Bonferroni-corrected significance threshold of P < 0.000000001. Understanding the biological mechanisms represented by these 19 proteins is important for clarifying how systemic molecular signals reflect neuronal injury, neuroimmune activation, and tissue-level remodeling in Parkinson disease. To address this question, we performed a comprehensive functional enrichment analysis of the 19 proteins, screening biological pathways across multiple knowledge resources, including Gene Ontology Molecular Function, Gene Ontology Biological Process, Gene Ontology Cellular Component, Reactome, and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes. Across these resources, we identified 59 significantly enriched biological pathways (Table 1). The enriched results indicate a convergent molecular landscape dominated by extracellular matrix organization, integrin-mediated adhesion, cell-surface signaling, phagocytosis, microglial immune activity, and broader stress-response and neuroendocrine processes. Representative signals include protein complex involved in cell adhesion with P = 4.227289989050737 × 10⁻⁷, external side of plasma membrane with P = 1.8623618097060346 × 10⁻⁶ and 7 associated proteins, integrin binding with P = 6.567673799645586 × 10⁻⁵, opsonin binding with P = 8.045019491307402 × 10⁻⁵, extracellular matrix organization with P = 0.0030007013052328036 and 5 associated proteins, and integrin cell surface interactions with P = 0.0006274793748841679. Together, these pathways suggest that Parkinson disease biology extends beyond neuron-intrinsic degeneration alone and includes altered neuron-microenvironment interactions, chronic inflammatory clearance mechanisms, adhesion-dependent signaling, and systemic neuroendocrine components. In the following sections, we examine the most significant pathways and their associated protein interaction patterns in greater detail to define the molecular framework captured by these disease-associated plasma proteins.

Pituitary gland (HPA:0370000)

The pituitary gland is the central endocrine effector of the hypothalamic-pituitary system and integrates neural signals from the hypothalamus into systemic hormonal outputs. It is composed of an anterior lobe and a posterior lobe, contains multiple specialized endocrine cell populations, and develops through tightly regulated morphogenetic and transcriptional programs that establish hormone-secreting lineages and enduring cellular networks required for coordinated secretory responses [1]. Through secretion of growth hormone, thyroid-stimulating hormone, adrenocorticotropic hormone, luteinizing hormone, follicle-stimulating hormone, prolactin, and posterior pituitary hormones released under hypothalamic control, the pituitary gland regulates growth, metabolism, stress responses, reproduction, fluid balance, and aspects of brain physiology. Its function depends on specialized vascular supply and close anatomical relations to the hypothalamus, which permit rapid neuroendocrine communication [2] [3]. In addition to its classical endocrine role, the pituitary gland is relevant to neurological disease because it is exposed to brain-derived signaling molecules, participates in growth hormone and insulin-like growth factor signaling that influence neuronal survival and brain function, and can harbor aggregated proteins including alpha-synuclein, tau, and amyloid-beta in both neurodegenerative disease and control tissues, indicating that it lies at an interface between endocrine regulation and proteinopathy-related biology [4] [5].

Against this biological background, the relationship between the pituitary gland pathway and Parkinson disease appears to be indirect but still biologically meaningful through three main mechanisms: neuroendocrine regulation, protein aggregation biology, and clinical complications of pituitary disease or its treatment. First, pituitary output contributes to systemic hormonal environments that may influence brain vulnerability. A recent review emphasized that growth hormone and insulin-like growth factor one, which are regulated by the anterior pituitary gland, act in the brain and that disturbances in their secretion or signaling may represent risk factors for neurodegenerative disorders including Parkinson disease [5]. This places the pituitary gland within a broader endocrine network that may modulate neuronal maintenance, repair, and resilience. Second, pathological alpha-synuclein deposits have been detected in human pituitary tissue, including in the adenohypophysis and neurohypophysis, showing that Parkinson-associated proteinopathy can involve the pituitary gland as a peripheral neuroendocrine site; however, the same study found no epidemiological evidence that recipients of cadaver-derived human growth hormone had increased risk of Parkinson disease, arguing against a clinically important human-to-human transmission route through pituitary-derived material [4]. This observation suggests that the pituitary gland may participate in the distribution or deposition of Parkinson-related proteins without serving as a demonstrated source of disease transmission. Third, pituitary pathology can produce parkinsonian syndromes that mimic Parkinson disease rather than reflect primary neurodegeneration. A case report described acute parkinsonism after transsphenoidal pituitary adenoma resection caused by extrapontine myelinolysis following treatment of hyponatremia, illustrating that disturbances linked to pituitary surgery and endocrine complications can generate reversible parkinsonian manifestations [6]. Additional indirect support comes from work localizing sperm protein twenty-two in pituitary somatotropes and thyrotropes; this protein has been linked to early-onset Parkinson disease biology, although the cited study did not establish a causal pituitary-specific mechanism for Parkinson disease [7]. Collectively, the literature indicates that the pituitary gland is not a core canonical Parkinson disease pathway in the same sense as nigrostriatal dopamine degeneration, but it remains relevant as a neuroendocrine modulator of disease susceptibility, a tissue in which Parkinson-associated alpha-synuclein pathology can be detected, and a clinical context in which secondary parkinsonism may arise.[1] [2] [3] [4] [5] [6] [7]

Adrenal gland; medullary cells[≥Low] (HPA:0020061)

The pathway described as adrenal gland medullary cells refers to the biology of the catecholamine-producing neuroendocrine cell population located in the inner portion of the adrenal gland. These medullary cells, commonly termed chromaffin cells, arise from neural crest-derived progenitors and are developmentally linked to Schwann cell precursor-like and sympathoadrenal lineages. Single-cell and single-nucleus studies show that the adrenal medulla is not a uniform compartment, but contains multiple transcriptionally distinct populations, including progenitor-like cells, neuroblast-like cells, and differentiated sympathoadrenal derivatives, indicating substantial developmental and functional heterogeneity within the medulla [14] [15] [16]. Developmental work further emphasizes that adrenal medullary formation depends on reciprocal interactions with the adrenal cortex and that medullary cells are integrated into broader stress-responsive endocrine circuitry [17]. Functionally, adrenal medullary cells synthesize and secrete catecholamines through regulated expression of enzymes such as tyrosine hydroxylase, dopamine beta-hydroxylase, and phenylethanolamine-N-methyltransferase, and this process is modulated by stress-related signaling pathways, including corticotropin-releasing factor and micro ribonucleic acid-mediated regulation [18]. Because these cells are neural crest-derived, catecholaminergic, stress-responsive, and capable of secreting trophic molecules under some conditions, they have long been considered biologically relevant to disorders involving dopaminergic degeneration and autonomic dysfunction, including Parkinson disease.

Against this biological background, the relationship between adrenal gland medullary cells and Parkinson disease is supported primarily by experimental transplantation and neurotrophic support studies, together with the broader biological overlap between adrenal medullary catecholamine cells and vulnerable nigrostriatal dopaminergic systems. Early preclinical work showed that grafts of adrenal medulla into Parkinsonian rat brain could reduce behavioral asymmetry, and the most effective graft responses were associated with restoration of striatal extracellular dopamine balance and partial normalization of dopamine receptor measures, indicating that adrenal medullary tissue can influence both presynaptic and postsynaptic dopaminergic function in vivo [19]. Mechanistic refinement came from studies using extra-adrenal chromaffin cells from the organ of Zuckerkandl, which improved motor deficits in a rat model of Parkinson disease despite not becoming dopaminergic neurons; instead, the grafted cells survived long term, promoted striatal reinnervation, increased dopamine content, and secreted glial cell line-derived neurotrophic factor together with transforming growth factor beta one, supporting the interpretation that adrenal medullary-related chromaffin cells may act through sustained trophic rescue rather than direct neurotransmitter replacement [20]. Broader reviews of neural transplantation in Parkinson disease also placed adrenal medullary tissue within the historical development of restorative strategies based on dopamine-secreting grafts and neurotrophic factor delivery [21]. In addition, the adrenal medulla is a central effector of stress biology, and stress-responsive signaling through the hypothalamus-pituitary-adrenal axis and sympathetic nervous system can shape neuroimmune states, including microglial activation patterns that are increasingly implicated in Parkinson disease pathogenesis [22]. Developmental and single-cell studies further strengthen biological plausibility by showing that adrenal medullary cells share neural crest origin, catecholaminergic differentiation programs, and progenitor relationships with sympathoadrenal lineages, features that make them relevant models for understanding catecholaminergic cell identity and resilience even if they are not themselves the primary degenerating neurons in Parkinson disease [14] [15] [17]. Taken together, the evidence indicates that this pathway is related to Parkinson disease not as a canonical disease-causing pathway, but as a therapeutically and mechanistically relevant catecholaminergic and neurotrophic system that may modulate dopaminergic survival, striatal function, and stress-linked neuroinflammatory processes.[19] [20] [21] [22] [14] [15] [17] [18] [16]

Focal adhesion (KEGG:04510)

Focal adhesion is a dynamic cell signaling and structural pathway that connects the extracellular matrix to the intracellular actin cytoskeleton through integrin receptors and a large multiprotein complex that includes focal adhesion kinase, paxillin, talin, vinculin, integrin linked kinase, and related adaptor and signaling proteins. This pathway serves not only as a physical anchoring system, but also as a central hub for mechanotransduction, allowing cells to sense extracellular matrix composition, stiffness, and force, and to convert these cues into biochemical programs that regulate adhesion, spreading, migration, survival, differentiation, and inflammatory behavior. The cited literature indicates that focal adhesion kinase acts as a key integrator of signals from cell adhesion and growth factors, and that mechanical force can directly induce conformational changes that activate focal adhesion kinase signaling, thereby linking extracellular mechanical inputs to intracellular responses [23]. Additional studies suggest that focal adhesion assembly and turnover are tightly coordinated with transcriptional regulators of cell mechanics, including yes associated protein, which can control expression of integrins and focal adhesion docking proteins and thereby influence adhesion strength, force generation, cell shape, and migration [24] [25]. The pathway also appears to be functionally coupled to calcium dependent mechanosensing, as force dependent recruitment of piezo type mechanosensitive ion channel component 1 to adhesions promotes adhesion maturation and calcium entry [26]. Taken together, focal adhesion may be viewed as a core pathway through which cells integrate extracellular matrix contact, cytoskeletal tension, and signaling cascades to regulate motility, tissue repair, and responses to stress.

Against this broader biological background, the most direct link between focal adhesion and Parkinson disease in the provided literature is the demonstration that the Parkinson disease associated leucine rich repeat kinase 2 Gly2019Ser mutation suppresses microglial motility by inhibiting focal adhesion kinase signaling [27]. In that study, mutant leucine rich repeat kinase 2 bound focal adhesion kinase, phosphorylated focal adhesion kinase at Thr X Arg or Lys motifs, reduced the activating autophosphorylation mark at tyrosine 397, and impaired adenosine diphosphate induced microglial movement and the rapid microglial containment response to brain injury; importantly, inhibition of leucine rich repeat kinase 2 kinase activity restored focal adhesion kinase activation and rescued motility defects [27]. This observation is particularly relevant in a Parkinson disease context because microglia are essential for surveillance, injury sensing, debris clearance, and orchestration of neuroinflammatory responses, suggesting that defective focal adhesion signaling could weaken protective glial reactions and thereby contribute to neurodegenerative progression. More broadly, the focal adhesion pathway regulates mechanotransduction, adhesion turnover, migration, and calcium signaling, all of which are likely to influence how microglia and other neural cells respond to tissue damage and changing extracellular matrix conditions in the diseased brain. The cited mechanistic studies further indicate that focal adhesion kinase is force responsive and serves as a central node translating extracellular mechanical cues into intracellular signaling [23], while yes associated protein dependent control of focal adhesion assembly and maturation governs cellular mechanics and persistent migration [24] [25]. In parallel, coupling between focal adhesions and piezo type mechanosensitive ion channel component 1 links adhesion dynamics to calcium entry [26], a feature that may be of interest because calcium dysregulation and maladaptive stress responses are widely implicated in neurodegeneration. Thus, although the provided publication set contains one principal Parkinson disease specific article, the broader focal adhesion literature supports a coherent and cautious disease model in which altered integrin to focal adhesion kinase signaling, impaired mechanosensing, defective adhesion turnover, and disturbed migration of microglia may reduce effective tissue repair and modify neuroinflammatory states in Parkinson disease, with leucine rich repeat kinase 2 driven inhibition of focal adhesion kinase representing the clearest disease specific mechanism identified here.[27] [23] [24] [25] [26]

ATP13A2 is among the most compelling proteins in a Parkinson’s disease context because it links lysosomal biology directly to a Mendelian form of parkinsonism. Loss-of-function mutations in ATP13A2 cause Kufor-Rakeb syndrome, a juvenile-onset parkinsonian disorder, strongly supporting a causal relationship between impaired lysosomal transport and dopaminergic neurodegeneration. Functionally, ATP13A2 contributes to lysosomal ion balance and polyamine transport, thereby sustaining lysosomal degradative capacity and autophagic flux. These processes are highly relevant to Parkinson’s disease because defective lysosomal clearance promotes the accumulation of misfolded proteins, including alpha-synuclein, and enhances mitochondrial and oxidative stress. Thus, ATP13A2 stands out as an important node connecting lysosomal dysfunction, impaired proteostasis, and alpha-synuclein pathology, all of which are central disease mechanisms in Parkinson’s disease.[28] [29] [30] [31]

From lysosomal ion balance and degradative capacity, the discussion naturally extends to another major lysosomal factor with strong genetic and clinical relevance in Parkinson disease.

GBA is one of the most clinically and biologically important Parkinson’s disease-associated proteins because heterozygous GBA variants are among the strongest genetic risk factors for the disorder. Glucocerebrosidase deficiency disrupts lysosomal sphingolipid degradation, leading to substrate accumulation, altered membrane composition, and impaired lysosomal function. This is particularly relevant to Parkinson’s disease because reduced GBA activity promotes alpha-synuclein aggregation, while alpha-synuclein itself can further inhibit glucocerebrosidase trafficking and function, creating a pathogenic feedback loop. The GBA pathway therefore provides a mechanistic bridge between lipid metabolism, lysosomal impairment, and synucleinopathy. Its importance is underscored by the fact that GBA-associated Parkinson’s disease often shows earlier onset and more severe cognitive involvement, making this protein highly relevant both mechanistically and translationally.[32] [33] [34] [35]

A closely related theme is intracellular trafficking and signaling control, which is captured by the next protein, leucine rich repeat kinase 2.

LRRK2 is a standout Parkinson’s disease protein because pathogenic variants in this kinase are among the most common causes of autosomal dominant Parkinson’s disease, and its molecular functions converge on several pathways central to neuronal homeostasis. LRRK2 phosphorylates Rab GTPases and thereby influences membrane trafficking, lysosomal positioning, endosomal maturation, and autophagic processes. These functions are highly relevant to Parkinson’s disease because defects in vesicle and lysosome biology can impair synaptic maintenance and protein clearance, ultimately favoring alpha-synuclein accumulation and neuroinflammation. In addition, LRRK2 activity has been linked to mitochondrial stress responses and immune-cell signaling, suggesting that it contributes not only to cell-autonomous neuronal vulnerability but also to inflammatory mechanisms that may accelerate disease progression. Because LRRK2 is enzymatically tractable, its disease relevance is further amplified by its therapeutic significance.[36] [37] [38] [39]

In turn, the emphasis on organelle quality control leads directly to mitochondrial surveillance pathways that have long been central to Parkinson disease research.

PINK1 has a direct and foundational connection to Parkinson’s disease because biallelic loss-of-function mutations cause autosomal recessive early-onset parkinsonism. Its best-established function is to accumulate on damaged mitochondria and activate Parkin, thereby marking dysfunctional organelles for selective autophagic removal. This pathway is particularly important in dopaminergic neurons, which are highly dependent on mitochondrial integrity because of their high energetic demands and vulnerability to oxidative stress. Failure of PINK1-mediated quality control leads to persistence of damaged mitochondria, increased reactive oxygen species, defective bioenergetics, and activation of inflammatory pathways. PINK1 therefore stands out as a central mechanistic link between mitochondrial quality control failure and neurodegeneration in Parkinson’s disease, and it is especially relevant because it operates directly upstream of Parkin itself.[40] [41] [42] [43]

Because PINK1 functions directly upstream of Parkin, the next protein follows logically from the same mitochondrial quality control framework.

PRKN is arguably the most directly relevant protein to Parkinson’s disease in this list because it encodes Parkin, one of the canonical genes responsible for autosomal recessive juvenile and early-onset parkinsonism. Parkin functions as an E3 ubiquitin ligase that is activated by PINK1 on damaged mitochondria, where it ubiquitinates numerous substrates to trigger mitophagy and broader mitochondrial quality-control programs. This activity is crucial for preventing the accumulation of dysfunctional mitochondria, oxidative injury, and metabolic insufficiency in neurons. Beyond mitophagy, Parkin also contributes to proteostasis and cellular stress adaptation, broadening its relevance to neurodegeneration.
