Introduction

Stroke (Disease code: I9_STROKE) is an acute cerebrovascular disorder characterized by the interruption of cerebral blood flow or rupture of cerebral vessels, leading to rapid neurological injury through mechanisms such as ischemia, hemorrhage, inflammation, and tissue remodeling. In a comprehensive analysis of the UK Biobank Pharma Proteomics Project data, circulating levels of 175 plasma proteins were significantly associated with stroke. This analysis included 50,797 controls and 1,392 cases, with significance determined using a stringent Bonferroni correction at a threshold of P < 0.000000001. Understanding the biological mechanisms represented by these proteins is crucial for elucidating the molecular basis of stroke, which involves not only vascular occlusion or bleeding but also a systemic response encompassing immune activation, extracellular matrix injury, cytokine signaling, and cellular stress adaptation. To further explore the functional implications of these proteins, a comprehensive functional enrichment analysis was conducted. This analysis utilized multiple pathway databases, including Gene Ontology Molecular Function, Gene Ontology Biological Process, Gene Ontology Cellular Component, Reactome, and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG). Across these resources, 70 significantly enriched biological pathways were identified. Notable pathways include cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction (P = 5.315 × 10⁻¹⁶), external encapsulating structure (P = 9.273 × 10⁻¹⁷), tumor necrosis factors binding their physiological receptors (P = 5.548 × 10⁻¹⁰), and regulation of leukocyte activation (P = 1.028 × 10⁻⁸). These pathways highlight key biological themes such as inflammatory and immune regulation, extracellular matrix and tissue barrier processes, and stress-response signaling, all of which are integral to the pathophysiology of stroke. The literature-supported evidence layer integrates 119 distinct PubMed references, ensuring that the narrative is not solely dependent on the primary proteomic association study. Key proteins such as TNF, CSF1, and TNFRSF1A are frequently mentioned in the literature, underscoring their relevance in stroke-related pathways. Furthermore, the STRING protein-protein interaction network analysis retained 94 high-confidence network edges among 46 proteins, with a mean interaction score of 0.88. Proteins such as TNF, EGFR, and CD4 emerged as highly connected nodes, suggesting that these proteins form coordinated interaction neighborhoods rather than isolated pathway annotations. Overall, these findings suggest that stroke is characterized by an integrated molecular framework involving cytokine signaling, leukocyte activation, extracellular structural disruption, protease regulation, and adaptive stress signaling. This comprehensive analysis provides a robust foundation for understanding the complex biological processes underlying stroke and sets the stage for the following sections, where we will delve deeper into the most significant pathways and their associated protein interactions.     3. Cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction (KEGG:04060)

    Agent Summary: The cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction pathway is a crucial communication network that facilitates the exchange of inflammatory and reparative signals among immune, vascular, glial, and neural cells. Pathway members (enrichment overlap): ACVRL1, CCL14, CCL15, CCL7, CD27, CD4. Context proteins (upstream regulators / disease context, not direct members): CD274. [203] [204] [205]
    Read Context ▽

    The cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction pathway is a core intercellular communication network that governs how immune, vascular, glial, and neural cells exchange inflammatory, reparative, and homeostatic signals. It comprises soluble ligands such as interleukins, chemokines, interferons, tumor necrosis factor family members, colony-stimulating factors, and growth factors, together with their cognate membrane receptors. Through ligand-receptor binding, this pathway initiates downstream signaling programs that regulate leukocyte recruitment, endothelial activation, blood-brain barrier integrity, microglial and macrophage polarization, neuronal survival or death, angiogenesis, thromboinflammation, and tissue remodeling. In the setting of cerebral ischemia, cytokine-receptor signaling is rapidly induced both within the ischemic brain and in the peripheral immune compartment. This pathway is therefore not a single linear cascade, but rather a signaling interface that integrates multiple biological processes central to stroke pathogenesis, including sterile inflammation, innate and adaptive immune activation, chemotaxis, vascular dysfunction, and post-ischemic repair. The publications provided consistently identify this pathway as one of the most significantly enriched pathways in ischemic stroke transcriptomic, epigenetic, non-coding ribonucleic acid, and single-cell analyses, indicating that dysregulated cytokine-receptor communication is a recurrent systems-level feature of stroke biology.

    To connect these pathway-level observations with the present enrichment results, it is useful to consider the number of stroke-associated proteins mapped to this pathway. There were 29 stroke-associated proteins significantly enriched in the cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction pathway, a central intercellular signaling system that coordinates inflammatory, vascular, glial, and neuronal communication during cerebral ischemia. This enrichment supports a quantitatively substantial involvement of cytokine-mediated ligand-receptor signaling in stroke biology, with the 29 proteins collectively mapping to a pathway that regulates leukocyte recruitment, endothelial activation, blood-brain barrier stability, microglial and macrophage functional polarization, neuronal injury responses, angiogenesis, thromboinflammation, and tissue remodeling. In the context of stroke, the concentration of 29 disease-associated proteins within this pathway suggests that dysregulated extracellular signaling may represent not a peripheral feature, but rather a core systems-level component of ischemic pathogenesis and post-ischemic repair.

    CCL14 stands out as potentially relevant to stroke because post-stroke injury is strongly shaped by chemokine gradients that govern leukocyte trafficking across the neurovascular unit. This receptor is unusual in that it does not primarily signal in the canonical chemokine-receptor manner; instead, it controls chemokine bioavailability by scavenging inflammatory chemokines and by supporting endothelial transcytosis and erythrocyte buffering of circulating chemokines. These properties are highly pertinent to ischemic stroke, where excessive recruitment of neutrophils and monocytes amplifies blood-brain barrier disruption, edema, and secondary tissue injury. By influencing the local and systemic availability of inflammatory chemokines such as C-C motif chemokine ligand 2, C-C motif chemokine ligand 5, C-X-C motif chemokine ligand 8, and related ligands, CCL14 may shape both the intensity and spatial distribution of post-ischemic inflammation. Its endothelial role is particularly notable because endothelial chemokine presentation is a key step in leukocyte adhesion and transmigration, processes central to reperfusion injury and infarct evolution.

    Closely related to this theme of chemokine-directed immune cell trafficking, CCL15 also appears potentially relevant in stroke. CCL15 is among the most compelling stroke-related candidates in this list because its annotated functions directly intersect with core mechanisms of ischemic brain injury: leukocyte recruitment, amplification of inflammation, and neuroinflammatory signaling. The protein contributes to the migration of monocytes, macrophages, T cells, dendritic cells, and neutrophils, all of which are major participants in post-stroke sterile inflammation. Particularly relevant is the reported mediation of neutrophil migration after C-C motif chemokine ligand 3 stimulation, accompanied by sequential tumor necrosis factor alpha and leukotriene B4 release, since these mediators are strongly implicated in endothelial activation, blood-brain barrier damage, and infarct expansion. The note that activation by C-C motif chemokine ligand 5 can drive neuroinflammation through extracellular signal-regulated kinase 1 and extracellular signal-regulated kinase 2 signaling further strengthens its relevance, as C-C motif chemokine ligand 5 and extracellular signal-regulated kinase pathway activation have both been linked to secondary neuronal injury after stroke. In addition, monocyte migration in response to C-X-C motif chemokine ligand 4 suggests a possible contribution to thrombo-inflammatory processes, which are central to ischemic stroke pathophysiology and vascular remodeling after injury. [219] [220] [221]

    Moving from chemokine biology to broader stress and inflammatory signaling, EDA2R may represent a more indirect but still potentially informative candidate. EDA2R is potentially relevant to stroke because it couples extracellular cues to nuclear factor kappa B and c-Jun N-terminal kinase signaling, two pathways that are central to ischemia-induced inflammation, stress responses, and cell death. Although its best-characterized biology is not stroke-specific, activation of tumor necrosis factor receptor associated factor-dependent nuclear factor kappa B and c-Jun N-terminal kinase cascades could plausibly influence endothelial activation, glial inflammatory responses, and neuronal injury in the ischemic brain. These signaling modules are repeatedly implicated in post-stroke cytokine production, apoptosis-related signaling, and secondary tissue damage. Therefore, EDA2R may be of interest as an upstream modulator of inflammatory and stress signaling, even if the present annotation provides less direct evidence for stroke involvement than the chemokine- or cytokine-related proteins in this dataset.

    In contrast to proteins that may amplify inflammatory injury, IL10RB appears more closely aligned with counter-regulatory immune signaling. IL10RB is highly relevant to stroke because it sits at the intersection of anti-inflammatory and innate immune signaling. Its role as an essential receptor component for interleukin 10 is particularly important, as interleukin 10 is a major immunoregulatory cytokine that can suppress excessive inflammatory activation after cerebral ischemia. In the context of stroke, controlled IL10RB-mediated signaling may help limit damaging cytokine production, leukocyte-mediated injury, and blood-brain barrier dysfunction. In parallel, its participation in type three interferon signaling through Janus kinase and signal transducer and activator of transcription and induction of interferon-stimulated genes suggests a broader role in post-stroke immune regulation, which is clinically relevant because stroke patients often develop systemic immune dysregulation and heightened susceptibility to infection. Thus, IL10RB may influence both neuroinflammatory injury within the brain and peripheral host defense after stroke, making it a particularly notable protein in this list.

    Shifting from immune regulation toward neuronal survival and repair, INHBB may be relevant in a different, more indirect manner. INHBB is of potential interest in stroke primarily because activin-related signaling has recognized links to tissue repair, inflammation, and neuronal survival. The annotation specifically mentions nerve cell survival, which makes this protein biologically relevant to ischemic brain injury, where neuronal death and attempted recovery are central outcomes. In addition, the broader roles of inhibin and activin family members in endocrine and metabolic regulation may be indirectly important, since metabolic state, insulin signaling, and systemic stress responses influence stroke risk and recovery. However, compared with the immune receptors and tumor necrosis factor family proteins in this dataset, the functional connection of INHBB to stroke is more indirect and likely relates to neuroprotection, repair, or systemic homeostatic responses rather than acute leukocyte-driven injury.

    Returning to stress-associated injury pathways, RELT may also warrant consideration. RELT is potentially relevant to stroke because apoptosis and stress-activated mitogen-activated protein kinase signaling are major determinants of secondary neuronal and glial injury after ischemia. The reported ability of RELT to activate p38 and c-Jun N-terminal kinase is noteworthy, as both pathways are strongly associated with inflammatory signaling, oxidative stress responses, and ischemia-induced cell death. The explicit suggestion that RELT may participate in apoptosis further supports a plausible role in infarct progression. Although the current annotation does not directly tie RELT to cerebrovascular disease, proteins that converge on p38, c-Jun N-terminal kinase, and apoptotic mechanisms are often mechanistically relevant in stroke, particularly during the subacute phase when programmed cell death and inflammatory amplification continue after the initial ischemic insult. [222] [223] [224] [225]

    A related extension of this theme is seen in tumor necrosis factor receptor family members that may couple inflammatory signaling to cell death programs. TNFRSF19 is relevant to stroke because it links tumor necrosis factor receptor biology to two central features of ischemic injury: inflammatory transcriptional activation and cell death. Activation of c-Jun and nuclear factor kappa B suggests the capacity to regulate stress-responsive and pro-inflammatory gene programs in neurons, glia, or vascular cells after ischemia. Its reported ability to promote caspase-independent cell death is also notable, as stroke pathology includes multiple non-apoptotic and caspase-independent death pathways in addition to classical apoptosis. The possibility that alternative isoforms act as decoy receptors introduces an additional layer of complexity, raising the prospect that TNFRSF19 could either exacerbate or buffer injury depending on isoform expression and tissue context. This makes it a plausible modulator of post-stroke inflammatory and degenerative signaling.

    Another tumor necrosis factor receptor family member with potentially broad relevance is TNFRSF6B. TNFRSF6B is particularly interesting in stroke because its annotated functions map onto several pathophysiological domains relevant to both acute injury and recovery. Nuclear factor kappa B activation may contribute to inflammatory amplification after ischemia, while promotion of caspase activation and apoptosis suggests a potential role in cell loss within ischemic tissue. Equally important is its inhibitory effect on vascular endothelial growth and angiogenesis in vitro. Angiogenesis is a key component of post-stroke repair and neurovascular remodeling, especially during recovery phases; therefore, a factor that suppresses endothelial growth could adversely affect tissue revascularization and functional recovery. Its effect on splenocyte alloactivation also points to broader immune-regulatory capacity, which is relevant because splenic and systemic immune responses substantially influence stroke outcomes.

    Finally, extending from innate and vascular signaling to adaptive immunity, TNFRSF9 may provide insight into later immune-mediated phases of stroke pathology. TNFRSF9 is relevant to stroke because it regulates T-cell activation and survival, processes increasingly recognized as important in post-stroke neuroinflammation. Although its canonical function is described in antiviral and antitumor immunity, enhancement of cluster of differentiation 8-positive T-cell survival and cytotoxicity may have implications for delayed immune-mediated tissue injury after cerebral ischemia. Cytotoxic lymphocytes can contribute to secondary neuronal damage and prolonged inflammatory remodeling in the injured brain. At the same time, because stroke also induces systemic immunosuppression, TNFRSF9-related pathways may affect the balance between harmful neuroinflammation and protective host defense. Thus, TNFRSF9 is mechanistically important as a marker of adaptive immune activation that could modulate stroke outcome depending on timing and immune context.

    4. Pleural mesothelioma (WP:WP5087)

    Agent Summary: Pleural mesothelioma is a malignant disease characterized by chronic inflammation, immune remodeling, and significant heterogeneity in tumor biology, which includes alterations in tumor suppressor genes and genomic instability. Pathway members (enrichment overlap): ACTA2, ANGPT2, AREG, CD274, COL4A1, CSF1. [176] [177] [178]
    Read Context ▽

    Pleural mesothelioma is a malignant disease arising from mesothelial cells of the pleura and is biologically characterized by chronic inflammation, diffuse local invasion along the pleural surface, marked intertumoral and intratumoral heterogeneity, and recurrent alterations in tumor suppressor programs such as BRCA1 associated protein 1, cyclin dependent kinase inhibitor 2A, and neurofibromin 2. Contemporary clinical and molecular studies indicate that pleural mesothelioma is not a single uniform entity, but rather a spectrum of diseases defined by histologic subtype, immune microenvironment, genomic instability, epigenetic state, and tumor cell morphology. Multiomic analyses have shown that adaptive immune response, methylation state, ploidy, and morphology are major axes of biological variation, while spatial profiling has demonstrated substantial heterogeneity across different pleural sites, including variation in extracellular matrix programs, cell adhesion pathways, and immune contexture. Therapeutically, pleural mesothelioma has been managed with combinations of surgery, radiotherapy, platinum plus pemetrexed chemotherapy, immune checkpoint blockade, and emerging targeted or metabolic strategies, although outcomes remain poor overall. Radical surgical approaches such as extrapleural pneumonectomy or extended pleurectomy decortication can produce substantial perioperative morbidity, whereas systemic treatments including nivolumab plus ipilimumab, durvalumab with platinum and pemetrexed, pegargiminase with chemotherapy, and enhancer of zeste homolog 2 inhibition reflect the increasing importance of tumor immunology and molecular stratification in this disease. Thus, the pleural mesothelioma pathway is best understood as an integrated oncologic program involving inflammation, coagulation perturbation, immune remodeling, tissue invasion, and treatment related systemic stress.

    Against this biological background, the possible relationship between pleural mesothelioma and stroke appears to be better explained by convergent clinical and systemic mechanisms than by a single shared canonical signaling pathway. The most direct evidence comes from a reported case in which pleural mesothelioma presented with acute ischemic stroke caused by internal carotid artery thrombotic occlusion; this patient showed thrombocytosis, hyperfibrinogenemia, and elevated C reactive protein, strongly suggesting that pleural mesothelioma can create a systemic prothrombotic and inflammatory state capable of precipitating large artery cerebral ischemia, and that restoration of carotid patency improved neurological outcome [176]. This observation is biologically plausible in light of broader mesothelioma literature describing the disease as an inflammation driven malignancy with substantial immune remodeling and extracellular matrix dysregulation, features that are also central to thrombosis, endothelial activation, and vascular injury. Multiomic and spatial studies further indicate that pleural mesothelioma exhibits marked heterogeneity in adaptive immune response and immune mediator expression, implying persistent systemic and local inflammatory signaling that could favor platelet activation and coagulation imbalance [177] [178]. In addition, several treatments used in pleural mesothelioma may modify stroke risk indirectly through cardiopulmonary toxicity, infection, or treatment related vascular complications. Major surgery for resectable disease is associated with high rates of serious adverse events, including cardiac and respiratory disorders and infections, all of which can amplify perioperative cerebrovascular risk in vulnerable patients [179] [180]. Systemic therapies also carry vascular relevance: pemetrexed based treatment has been noted to require monitoring for rare but severe adverse effects including stroke, while immune checkpoint blockade has produced severe immune related toxicities such as encephalitis and heart failure, which reflect the capacity of these regimens to induce systemic inflammatory injury with potential neurological consequences [181] [182]. Collectively, the available evidence suggests that pleural mesothelioma may be related to stroke primarily through cancer associated hypercoagulability, inflammation driven endothelial and platelet activation, and treatment associated systemic complications rather than through a stroke specific molecular pathway intrinsic to the tumor alone. [176] [177] [178] [179] [180] [181] [182]

    MDK is the most clearly stroke-relevant protein in this list because its annotated functions map directly onto several core mechanisms of ischemic and hemorrhagic brain injury. First, MDK is a potent regulator of inflammation: it promotes neutrophil and macrophage recruitment to sites of injury and can amplify chemokine expression, while also shaping adaptive immunity by suppressing tolerogenic dendritic cell development and promoting T-cell expansion and T helper 1 differentiation. These activities are highly pertinent to stroke, in which post-ischemic neuroinflammation contributes both to acute tissue damage and to later repair processes [183] [184] [185]. Second, MDK has strong prosurvival and regenerative properties. It activates anti-apoptotic mitogen activated protein kinase and protein kinase B signaling, promotes tissue regeneration after injury, and supports survival and growth of neural precursor cells and embryonic neurons, suggesting a plausible role in limiting neuronal death and supporting recovery after cerebral ischemia [186] [187] [185]. Third, MDK is relevant to post-stroke neurovascular remodeling because it enhances vascular endothelial proliferation, induces vascular endothelial growth factor A expression and nitric oxide synthase activity, and contributes to vasodilation and arteriogenesis, all of which are mechanistically linked to reperfusion, collateral circulation, and repair of the neurovascular unit. In addition, its ability to promote cell migration, epithelial to mesenchymal transition, and smooth muscle cell migration suggests a role in vascular remodeling after injury, which may be beneficial for recovery but could also contribute to pathological remodeling depending on context [188]. Finally, MDK directly participates in neuronal processes central to stroke recovery, including neuronal migration, neurite outgrowth, cell adhesion, and oligodendroglial precursor-like cell elongation, indicating potential involvement in axonal plasticity and remyelination during the subacute and chronic phases of stroke. Taken together, MDK appears to be a multifunctional mediator positioned at the intersection of neuroinflammation, neuronal survival, angiogenesis, and tissue repair, which may make it particularly relevant to stroke pathobiology and recovery.     ACTA2 is potentially relevant to stroke because actin cytoskeletal dynamics are fundamental to vascular smooth muscle contraction, cell migration, and structural remodeling of the vessel wall. Although the provided annotation is general, ACTA2 is especially notable in the cerebrovascular context because smooth muscle cell contractility influences vascular tone, cerebral blood flow regulation, and the integrity of larger arteries and arterioles. These processes are directly relevant to stroke susceptibility and outcome, particularly in relation to vessel narrowing, hemodynamic instability, and post-injury vascular remodeling. However, compared with MDK, the stroke connection is less specifically supported by the supplied functional description, which only establishes a broad role in cell motility rather than explicitly linking ACTA2 to inflammation, neuronal survival, angiogenesis, or brain repair. Therefore, ACTA2 should be regarded as a plausible but less strongly prioritized stroke-related candidate based on the current annotation.

    5. Matrix metalloproteinases (WP:WP129)

    Agent Summary: Matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) are zinc-dependent enzymes that play a crucial role in remodeling the extracellular matrix, impacting processes such as synaptic remodeling, neuronal plasticity, and angiogenesis in the central nervous system. Core family members: MMP12, MMP7, TIMP1, BSG, TNF. [135] [136] [72]
    Read Context ▽

    Matrix metalloproteinases are a family of zinc-dependent endopeptidases that remodel the extracellular matrix by cleaving structural proteins, basement membrane components, cell-surface receptors, adhesion molecules, and signaling mediators. In the central nervous system, these enzymes participate in physiological processes that include synaptic remodeling, neuronal plasticity, angiogenesis, and tissue repair, but their activity appears to require tight control by endogenous tissue inhibitors of metalloproteinases as well as by transcriptional, post-translational, and redox-dependent mechanisms. Within the neurovascular unit, matrix metalloproteinases regulate the integrity of the vascular basement membrane and intercellular junctional complexes, including occludin and claudin-5, thereby influencing blood-brain barrier permeability. Different family members also show partially distinct cellular sources and temporal patterns after injury. Matrix metalloproteinase-9 has been strongly linked to leukocytes and vascular compartments during acute injury, whereas matrix metalloproteinase-2 has also been associated with vascular remodeling and later reparative responses. Other members, including matrix metalloproteinase-3, matrix metalloproteinase-7, and matrix metalloproteinase-8, may contribute in cell-type-specific and context-dependent ways. Taken together, this pathway may be viewed as a dynamic extracellular proteolytic system whose effects can shift between protective and destructive depending on magnitude, timing, anatomical compartment, and disease stage.

    Against this mechanistic background, the collected literature suggests that the matrix metalloproteinase pathway is centrally involved in stroke pathobiology because it connects ischemic injury, neurovascular barrier failure, inflammation, hemorrhagic conversion, and later recovery. In the acute phase of ischemic stroke, increased matrix metalloproteinase activity, especially matrix metalloproteinase-9 and to a lesser extent matrix metalloproteinase-2, promotes degradation of extracellular matrix and vascular basement membrane, disrupts tight junction proteins, increases blood-brain barrier permeability, and facilitates leukocyte infiltration, edema, infarct expansion, and hemorrhagic transformation, including complications associated with tissue plasminogen activator treatment. These relationships are repeatedly highlighted in mechanistic and translational studies and are further supported by experimental observations showing positive correlations between matrix metalloproteinase-9 or matrix metalloproteinase-2 expression and infarct volume, swelling, hemorrhage, and neurological disability, while tissue plasminogen activator exposure and longer ischemia appear to amplify these responses. Upstream inflammatory regulators also converge on this pathway, as extracellular matrix metalloproteinase inducer promotes matrix metalloproteinase production and is associated with worse outcome, whereas interventions such as apelin-13, memantine, hydrogen sulfide-related signaling, hyperoxia in selected settings, and mesenchymal stem cell therapy have been reported to attenuate injury in parallel with suppression of excessive matrix metalloproteinase activation.     6. Interleukin-10 signaling (REAC:R-HSA-6783783)

    Agent Summary: Interleukin-10 signaling is a crucial anti-inflammatory and immunoregulatory pathway that plays a significant role in stroke by limiting excessive immune activation and promoting tissue repair. Core family members: IL10RB, CD80, CSF1, TIMP1, TNF, TNFRSF1A. [124] [125] [126]
    Read Context ▽

    Interleukin ten signaling is a canonical anti-inflammatory and immunoregulatory cytokine pathway that limits excessive innate and adaptive immune activation while supporting tissue protection and repair. Interleukin ten is produced by several cell types relevant to stroke, including regulatory T cells, macrophages, microglia, monocytes, and, in some contexts, B cells. After binding to the interleukin ten receptor complex on target cells, the pathway activates intracellular signaling centered on signal transducer and activator of transcription three, leading to transcriptional programs that suppress pro-inflammatory cytokine production, restrain nuclear factor kappa B driven responses, reduce antigen-presenting and cytotoxic immune activity, and promote a reparative immune phenotype. In the injured brain, this signaling pathway may influence microglial and macrophage polarization, reduce secondary inflammatory damage, modulate lymphocyte effector programs, and support white matter preservation. The pathway is also linked to metabolic and redox regulation, as interleukin ten signaling can reduce lipid reactive oxygen species accumulation and inhibit ferroptotic cell death in oligodendrocyte progenitor cells through interleukin ten receptor and signal transducer and activator of transcription three dependent mechanisms. Because stroke evolves through temporally dynamic phases that include acute ischemic injury, inflammatory expansion, tissue clearance, and later remodeling, interleukin ten signaling is perhaps best understood as a context-dependent protective pathway whose beneficial effects may depend on timing, cellular source, target cell population, and the balance between local neuroprotection and systemic immunosuppression.

    Against this biological background, the collected literature suggests that interleukin ten signaling is closely related to stroke because it functions as a major endogenous brake on post-stroke inflammation while also contributing to repair and recovery. Broad reviews of post-stroke immunity identify interleukin ten among the key cytokines that shape the inflammatory response after ischemic injury, emphasizing that inflammation can be both harmful and beneficial depending on disease stage and that manipulation of interleukin ten therefore has therapeutic promise but also translational complexity [124]. Mechanistic experimental evidence supports a protective role: mice lacking interleukin ten develop larger infarcts, greater brain atrophy, and worse neurological outcome after transient middle cerebral artery occlusion, whereas recombinant interleukin ten improves outcome by restraining the harmful interleukin seventeen A response in the ischemic brain; this regulation involves direct control of cluster of differentiation four positive alpha beta T cells and indirect control of gamma delta T cells through regulatory T cells, establishing interleukin ten signaling as a critical checkpoint of lymphocyte-mediated injury after stroke [125]. A more recent study further shows that regulatory T cells are required for exercise-enhanced post-stroke recovery because they suppress persistent neuronal hyperexcitability in peri-infarct tissue through interleukin ten signaling, linking this pathway not only to immunomodulation but also to circuit stabilization and functional recovery [126]. Beyond immune cell crosstalk, interleukin ten directly protects vulnerable glial populations after hemorrhagic stroke: it activates interleukin ten receptor and signal transducer and activator of transcription three signaling in oligodendrocyte progenitor cells, induces delta like non-canonical Notch ligand one, adenosine monophosphate activated protein kinase, and acetyl coenzyme A carboxylase related metabolic reprogramming, lowers lipid reactive oxygen species, prevents ferroptosis, and thereby preserves myelin integrity and long-term cognitive function [127]. Several intervention studies are consistent with this protective framework, showing that treatments associated with improved stroke outcome also increase interleukin ten or interleukin ten linked anti-inflammatory programs, including promotion of reparative microglial polarization by muscone and phoenixin twenty, reduction of nuclear factor kappa B driven inflammation by metformin, and beneficial immunomodulation after repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation and early rehabilitation training, all of which were accompanied by higher interleukin ten levels or stronger anti-inflammatory phenotypes in experimental or clinical stroke settings [128] [129] [130] [131] [132]. At the same time, the pathway appears to have a clinically important duality: acute ischemic stroke is associated with elevated circulating interleukin ten together with monocyte dysfunction and altered B cell responses, suggesting that excessive or systemic interleukin ten linked immunoregulation may contribute to stroke-induced immunosuppression and susceptibility to infection outside the brain [133] [134]. Overall, the available evidence supports the view that interleukin ten signaling is a central determinant of stroke biology, limiting inflammatory tissue damage, suppressing pathogenic lymphocyte responses, promoting reparative microglial states, protecting white matter, and facilitating neurological recovery, while also requiring careful temporal and compartment-specific therapeutic targeting to avoid unwanted systemic immune suppression.     7. MAPK signaling pathway (KEGG:04010)

    Agent Summary: The mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) signaling pathway is a conserved network that translates extracellular signals into cellular responses, playing a critical role in regulating cell survival, inflammation, oxidative stress, and neurogenesis.     The mitogen-activated protein kinase signaling pathway is a highly conserved intracellular signal transduction network that converts extracellular stress signals, inflammatory mediators, growth factors, cytokines, and receptor activation events into coordinated transcriptional and post-transcriptional cellular responses. This pathway is classically organized into kinase cascades in which upstream membrane-associated signals activate sequential protein kinases, culminating in the activation of major branches centered on extracellular signal-regulated kinase, c-Jun N-terminal kinase, and p38 mitogen-activated protein kinase. Through these branches, the pathway regulates cell survival and death, inflammatory gene expression, oxidative stress responses, mitochondrial function, cytoskeletal remodeling, blood-brain barrier integrity, glial activation, immune cell behavior, neurogenesis, and synaptic plasticity. In the nervous system, the biological output of mitogen-activated protein kinase signaling is context dependent: moderate or temporally restricted activation may support repair, plasticity, and trophic signaling, whereas excessive or sustained activation under ischemic conditions promotes neuronal apoptosis, inflammasome activation, reactive oxygen species accumulation, mitochondrial dysfunction, and secondary tissue injury. In stroke, this pathway appears to function as a central hub that integrates ischemia-reperfusion stress with neuroinflammatory and neurovascular responses, and it is extensively modulated by microRNAs, long non-coding RNAs, growth factor receptors, immune kinases, and pharmacological interventions.

    Against this biological background, the collected literature suggests that the mitogen-activated protein kinase signaling pathway is a major mechanistic driver of stroke pathobiology, particularly in ischemic stroke and its neuropsychiatric and systemic complications. Multiple studies indicate that cerebral ischemia and reperfusion induce overactivation of extracellular signal-regulated kinase, c-Jun N-terminal kinase, and p38 mitogen-activated protein kinase, which in turn amplifies oxidative stress, mitochondrial injury, inflammatory cytokine production, neuronal apoptosis, and cognitive impairment. Experimental suppression of this pathway repeatedly confers neuroprotection. For example, silencing activating transcription factor 3 reduced infarction, neurological injury, reactive oxygen species production, and mitochondrial dysfunction while lowering extracellular signal-regulated kinase phosphorylation, and these benefits were reversed by pharmacological activation of mitogen-activated protein kinase signaling, directly supporting a causal role in ischemic injury. Consistently, microRNA-mediated inhibition of Src-dependent mitogen-activated protein kinase signaling by microRNA-137, as well as by microRNA-203a-3p and microRNA-153-3p, attenuated inflammation, oxidative stress, apoptosis, neuronal damage, and post-stroke cognitive deficits. Long non-coding RNA maternally expressed gene 3 promoted ischemic injury by suppressing microRNA-424-5p and engaging semaphorin 3A-associated mitogen-activated protein kinase signaling, whereas its inhibition reduced infarct volume and improved neurological outcome. Beyond neurons, the pathway also regulates immune and glial injury after stroke: hematopoietic progenitor kinase 1 enhanced neutrophil hyperactivation and post-stroke lung and brain injury through nuclear factor kappa-light-chain-enhancer of activated B cells, signal transducer and activator of transcription 3, and p38 mitogen-activated protein kinase signaling; glycoprotein nonmetastatic melanoma protein B limited neuroinflammation by suppressing p38, c-Jun N-terminal kinase, and extracellular signal-regulated kinase activation; and Strawberry notch homolog 1-driven neural stem cell extracellular vesicles improved recovery by restraining microglial nuclear factor kappa-light-chain-enhancer of activated B cells and mitogen-activated protein kinase signaling. Several therapeutic studies further reinforce the translational relevance of this pathway, showing that traditional medicines, anhydrosafflor yellow B, beta-bisabolene, sulbutiamine, and herbal extracts reduce ischemic damage in association with inhibition of mitogen-activated protein kinase signaling and downstream apoptotic or inflammatory programs. In addition, pathway-centered network analyses link insulin-like growth factor 1 receptor and the mitogen-activated protein kinase pathway to post-stroke depression, and dietary inulin appears to modulate post-stroke depressive-like behavior through insulin-like growth factor 1-mediated mitogen-activated protein kinase signaling. Taken together, these data support the view that the mitogen-activated protein kinase signaling pathway is not merely a downstream marker of ischemic stress but may represent a broader integrative axis connecting excitotoxicity, oxidative damage, mitochondrial failure, neuroinflammation, immune cell recruitment, neuronal death, functional recovery, and post-stroke depression, making it a potentially compelling mechanistic and therapeutic target in stroke.     8. antigen processing and presentation of peptide antigen via MHC class I (GO:0002474)

    Agent Summary: the immune response. This could contribute to secondary brain damage through autoimmune-like mechanisms, where the immune system mistakenly targets and destroys healthy brain tissue. Pathway members (enrichment overlap): B2M, HLA-E, IFI30, ULBP2. Context proteins (upstream regulators / disease context, not direct members): EDA2R, LTBR, RELT, TNF, TNFRSF10A. [14] [15] [16]
    Read Context ▽

    Antigen processing and presentation of peptide antigen via major histocompatibility complex class one is a core cellular immune pathway through which intracellular proteins are degraded into short peptides, loaded onto classical major histocompatibility complex class one molecules, and displayed at the cell surface for surveillance by cluster of differentiation eight positive cytotoxic T lymphocytes. In the canonical route, endogenous proteins are cleaved mainly by the proteasome, including the immunoproteasome under inflammatory conditions, and the resulting peptides are transported into the endoplasmic reticulum by transporter associated with antigen processing, where peptide loading complexes support assembly of major histocompatibility complex class one heavy chain with beta two microglobulin and antigenic peptide before export through the Golgi apparatus to the plasma membrane. The pathway is highly regulated at multiple levels, including transcriptional control of major histocompatibility complex class one molecules and antigen processing machinery by inflammatory signaling such as nuclear factor kappa B and histone acetyltransferases p300 and CREB binding protein, as demonstrated in cancer cells where activation of these regulators increases major histocompatibility complex class one expression, immunoproteasome components, and peptide transport machinery [14]. Beyond direct presentation of endogenous antigens, professional antigen presenting cells, especially dendritic cells, can route exogenous material into major histocompatibility complex class one presentation through cross-presentation, using either cytosolic or vacuolar processing routes and specialized intracellular trafficking steps involving post Golgi compartments and phagosomal export machinery [15] [16] [17] [18] [19]. The pathway is also sensitive to perturbation by infection, cellular stress, and organelle dysfunction: viral proteins can suppress peptide loading or surface display, whereas endoplasmic reticulum stress and Golgi fragmentation can reduce peptide major histocompatibility complex class one complex presentation [20] [21] [22]. Collectively, this pathway links intracellular proteostasis, inflammatory signaling, organelle homeostasis, and adaptive immune activation, thereby shaping whether damaged, infected, transformed, or stressed cells become visible to cytotoxic lymphocytes.

    Against this mechanistic background, the pathway may be cautiously considered in relation to stroke, where sterile injury and immune activation occur in parallel over time. Although the provided publications do not study stroke directly, they define mechanisms that are highly relevant to stroke pathobiology because stroke induces sterile inflammation, neuronal and vascular cell stress, blood brain barrier disruption, antigen release, and subsequent activation of both innate and adaptive immunity. In ischemic or hemorrhagic brain injury, damaged neurons, astrocytes, oligodendrocytes, endothelial cells, microglia, infiltrating macrophages, and dendritic cell like antigen presenting cells are exposed to inflammatory cytokines and danger signals that are expected to enhance major histocompatibility complex class one antigen processing and presentation machinery, particularly through pathways involving nuclear factor kappa B, signal transducer and activator of transcription one, and immunoproteasome induction. This inference is supported by studies showing that inflammatory transcriptional programs can strongly increase major histocompatibility complex class one antigen presentation capacity, including upregulation of major histocompatibility complex class one molecules, immunoproteasome subunits, and peptide transport machinery [14] [23] [24]. In the post stroke brain, such enhancement would be expected to increase display of self peptides derived from injured intracellular proteins, thereby facilitating recognition by cytotoxic T lymphocytes and potentially amplifying secondary tissue injury. At the same time, cross-presentation mechanisms characterized in dendritic cells provide a plausible route by which brain derived antigens released from dying cells are captured in cervical lymphoid tissues or by infiltrating antigen presenting cells and then presented on major histocompatibility complex class one to prime cytotoxic T cell responses [15] [16] [17] [18] [19]. This process is particularly relevant to delayed neuroinflammation after stroke, when peripheral adaptive immunity contributes to lesion evolution and repair. The publications also indicate that endoplasmic reticulum stress and Golgi disorganization suppress major histocompatibility complex class one peptide display [21], a finding of special importance in stroke because ischemia, reperfusion, oxidative stress, and excitotoxicity profoundly disturb endoplasmic reticulum and Golgi homeostasis; thus, stroke may produce spatially and temporally heterogeneous antigen presentation, with some cells becoming more immunogenic through inflammatory induction while others lose efficient peptide loading because of severe organelle injury. In addition, studies of viral immune evasion and peptide loading complex arrest illustrate how disruption of transporter associated with antigen processing dependent loading changes cell surface major histocompatibility complex class one expression [22] [20], which is conceptually relevant because similar defects could emerge in severely injured brain cells after stroke. Overall, this pathway is related to stroke through its central role in converting intracellular damage caused by cerebral ischemia or hemorrhage into adaptive immune recognition: it can promote cytotoxic lymphocyte activation against brain antigens, shape the magnitude and persistence of post stroke neuroinflammation, and influence the balance between harmful immune mediated secondary injury and beneficial clearance of damaged tissue.
